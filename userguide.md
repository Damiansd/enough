### Part 1: Hello, *enough*!

## Chapter 1: What is *enough*? {#what-is-enough}

*enough* is a helper that lives on your computer. It answers one big question:

> **"Will our money last for the rest of our lives?"**

That's where the name comes from. It helps you find out if you have *enough*.

### What it does

You tell *enough* about your money: your pay, your super, your house, your loan and what you
spend. Then *enough* looks into the future, **one month at a time**, all the way until the youngest
person in your home is 100.

For every month, it works out:

- the money that comes in, like pay, super payments and the Age Pension
- the money that goes out, like spending, tax and the house loan
- how much is left in each money jar at the end

It knows the Australian rules for tax, super, the Age Pension and aged care, so you don't have to.

### What you can do with it

- **Pick a day to stop working** and see if the money lasts.
- **Find out how much you can spend** each year.
- **Play "what if?"** Make a pretend future, change one thing, and see the difference.
- **Keep track of your spending** by bringing in your bank lists.
- **Ask it questions** using the chat.

It works for one person on their own, or for a couple.

### What it doesn't do

- **It doesn't move any real money.** Nothing you do in *enough* touches your bank or your super.
  It's like playing shops with pretend money.
- **It doesn't connect to your bank.** You bring your bank lists in yourself (see [Chapter 34](#csv-import)).
- **It doesn't tell you what to do.** It shows you what would happen. You and your family decide.
  For big choices, talk to a financial adviser too.
- **It can't see the future for sure.** It's a careful guess, not a promise (see [Chapter 6](#guess-not-promise)).

### Where your information lives

Everything you type stays **on your own computer**. Nobody else can see it.

There's one exception: **the chat**. When you use the chat, your question and the figures it needs
are sent over the internet so it can answer. [Chapter 57](#chat-setup) explains this before you turn it on.

### How to use this book

You don't have to read this book from start to finish. Jump to whatever you need.

- **Got a question?** Try the **[Which chapter do I need?](#which-chapter)** page next.
- **Looking at a box on the screen and wondering what it is?** Try Part 5, *A Walk Around the Screen*.
- **Found a word you don't know?** Look in the dictionary near the end.

In every chapter, you'll see our pretend family, **Pip and Jo**. You'll meet them in
[Chapter 2](#pip-and-jo).

## Chapter 2: Meet Pip and Jo {#pip-and-jo}

Pip and Jo are a pretend couple. They are not real people. We made them up so that every chapter
can show you what *enough* does to a real-looking family.

### Who they are

Pip is **55**. Jo is **53**. They live together in a house they are still paying off.

They both go to work. Pip works full time. Jo works part time, so Jo earns less.

### Their money jars

A good way to think about money is **jars**, each holding money for a different job.
[Chapter 4](#money-jars) explains all of them. For now, here are Pip and Jo's.

- **Pip's super jar** has **{{fig pip-super-today nearest-1|$500,000}}** in it. Super is money saved for when you're older, and
  you can't use it until then. Pip has worked a long time, so it is big.
- **Jo's super jar** has **{{fig jo-super-today nearest-1|$50,000}}** in it. Jo has worked fewer hours, so it is small.
- Each super jar takes out **a small fee** every year for looking after the money (see [Chapter 10](#super-fees)).
- **The offset account** has **{{fig offset-today nearest-1|$10,000}}** in it. This is a savings jar that sits next to the house
  loan and makes the loan cost a little less.
- **The house loan** is **{{fig loan-today nearest-1|$250,000}}**. They pay **{{fig repayment-today nearest-1|$2,300}}** every month to shrink it.

### What they spend

Pip and Jo spend about **{{fig spend-from-now nearest-1|$70,000}} a year**. That is for food, bills, the car, holidays and fun.

### Their big question

Pip and Jo want to know one thing:

> **"If we both stop working in January 2034, will our money last for the rest of our lives?"**

In January 2034, Pip will be 63 and Jo will be 61.

### Their plan

Pip and Jo have a plan. Each step has a date, and *enough* keeps track of all of them.

1. **Next year, they move house.** Their new house costs more, so their loan grows to {{fig loan-after-move nearest-1|$350,000}}.
2. **In January 2034, they both stop working.**
3. **When Pip is 67, they pay off the house loan** using some of their savings.
4. **As they get older, they spend a bit less.** They plan to spend {{fig spend-from-67 nearest-1|$75,000}} a year at 67, then
   less and less, down to {{fig spend-from-85 nearest-1|$50,000}} a year from 85.

*enough* takes this plan and guesses what will happen every month, all the way until Jo is 100.
It keeps going until the younger one is 100, in case one of them lives much longer than the other.

### How you'll see Pip and Jo in this book

In lots of chapters, Pip and Jo try something new. For example, "What if Pip works one more year?"
or "What if they sell the house?"

These are **what-ifs**. A what-if is like a pretend game. It never changes the facts about Pip and
Jo. When a chapter is finished, Pip and Jo go back to how they were.

All their facts live on one page: **[Chapter 83: Pip and Jo's facts](#pip-and-jo-facts)**.
If you ever forget how much is in a jar, look there.

## Chapter 3: Grown-ups stop working one day {#retiring}

Most grown-ups go to work to earn money. One day, they stop. This is called **retiring**.

When you retire, your pay stops. But you still need money for food, bills and fun, maybe for
thirty years or more. So where does it come from?

### Where the money comes from after work stops

1. **Super.** While you work, a little of your pay goes into a special jar called super. You can't
   touch it until you're older. When you retire, it starts paying you, a bit like pay.
2. **Savings and investments.** Any money you've saved outside super.
3. **The Age Pension.** Money from the government for older people who don't have a lot.
   It usually starts at 67 (see [Chapter 48](#age-pension)).

The big worry is simple: **what if the money runs out before you do?** That's the question
*enough* helps you answer.

### Pip and Jo

Pip and Jo plan to stop working together, in January 2034. Pip will be 63 and Jo will be 61.

That means Pip and Jo will be retired for a long time before the Age Pension could start. They will
need their super to pay for everything in those years. In [Chapter 17](#retire-date), you'll see what
*enough* says about that.

## Chapter 4: Money in jars {#money-jars}

A good way to think about money is **jars**. Each jar holds money for a different job. *enough*
keeps track of every jar, every month.

### The jars

- **Super jar.** Money for when you're older. Your job puts some in for you, and you can put in
  extra. It grows over time, but it's locked until you're older and have stopped working. Each
  person has their own super jar.
- **Savings jar.** Money in the bank that you can use any time. If you have a house loan, this is
  often an **offset account**: a savings jar joined to the loan. The money in it is still yours,
  but it makes the loan cost less.
- **Investment jar.** Things like shares, which you buy hoping they'll grow. You can sell them
  when you need money, but you may pay tax when you do (see [Chapter 30](#capital-gains-and-franking)).
- **The house.** Your home is worth a lot, but it's a funny sort of jar. You live in it, so you
  can't spend it unless you sell it or borrow against it (see Way 7).
- **The house loan.** This is an upside-down jar. It's money you **owe**. Every payment makes it
  smaller.

### Money moving between jars

Every month, money moves around. Pay goes in. Spending comes out. Super pays you when you retire.
The loan gets smaller.

*enough* follows all of this for you. If the savings jar runs low, it knows which jar to take money
from next.

## Chapter 5: Pretend money and real money {#real-and-pretend-money}

Here's a strange thing about money: **a dollar buys less every year.**

When your grandparents were young, an ice-cream might have cost 20 cents. Now it costs a few
dollars. The ice-cream didn't get better. Prices went up. This is called **inflation**.

### Why this matters for a forecast

*enough* looks a long way into the future, fifty years or more. Over that time, prices go up a
lot. So a number like "{{fig spend-from-now-in-2056 nearest-1000|$147,000}} in 2056" is hard to understand. Is that a lot? Is it a little?

It helps to turn future money back into **today's money**: what it would buy **right now**.

### Pip and Jo's spending

Pip and Jo spend {{fig spend-from-now nearest-1|$70,000}} a year. By 2056, prices will have gone up so much that the same food,
bills and fun will cost about **{{fig spend-from-now-in-2056 nearest-1000|$147,000}}**.

So which number is right? **Both.**

- **{{fig spend-from-now-in-2056 nearest-1000|$147,000}}** is the **real future amount**: the actual dollars they'll pay in 2056.
- **{{fig spend-from-now nearest-1|$70,000}}** is the same thing in **today's money**: what that shopping would cost right now.

### How *enough* shows money

*enough* can show every number either way, with a switch at the top of the page:

- **"{{name|Today’s money — what it would buy now}}".** This is what you see first. It's easier to compare.
- **"{{name|The actual future amounts}}".** The real dollars on the day.

[Chapter 61](#todays-money-switch) shows you the switch. If a number ever looks much smaller or bigger than you expected,
check which one you're looking at.

### How much do prices go up?

Nobody knows for sure. *enough* uses a guess, which you can change (see [Chapter 15](#assumptions)). For Pip and
Jo, the guess is that prices go up by **2.5% a year**. That means something costing $100 today
costs about {{fig hundred-dollars-in-ten-years nearest-1|$128}} in ten years.

**Where this comes from:** how much prices have gone up so far is measured by the [Consumer Price Index (ABS)](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/economy/price-indexes-and-inflation/consumer-price-index-australia). The 2.5% a year for the future is a guess, not an ABS figure. In the appendix: [Prices and wages](#sources-prices-and-wages).

## Chapter 6: It's a guess, not a promise {#guess-not-promise}

*enough* is very careful. It knows the rules, and it does the sums for every single month. But it
still can't see the future. Its answer is a **forecast**: a careful guess.

### Why it can't be sure

- **Investments go up and down.** In real life, some years are good and some are bad. *enough*
  uses one steady guess instead: for Pip and Jo, their investments grow by 6.5% every year. Real
  life will be bumpier.
- **Prices and pay can change faster or slower** than the guess.
- **The government changes the rules.** Tax, super and the Age Pension rules change often.
  *enough* uses today's rules for the whole future (see [Chapter 79](#rules-updates)).
- **Life happens.** People get sick, change jobs, or live longer than they expected.

### So what is it good for?

A forecast is great for **comparing choices**.

"What if Pip works one more year?" *enough* can show you the difference that makes. Even if the
exact numbers turn out wrong, the difference between two choices is usually a good guide.

### Good habits

- **Look at the difference, not just the number.** Compare plans side by side (see [Chapter 40](#comparing-scenarios)).
- **Update your numbers** every so often, maybe once a year.
- **Use it to ask better questions.** For big choices, take what you learn to a financial adviser.

## Chapter 7: Starting fresh {#starting-fresh}

The first time you open *enough*, it has no information about you. So it asks just two questions.

### Opening *enough*

*enough* opens in your web browser, just like a website. But this website lives on **your own
computer**, not on the internet. The person who set up *enough* for you will show you how to open
it.

### The welcome box

You'll see a box that says **"{{name|Welcome — let’s start your plan}}"**. It asks for:

1. **{{name|Your name}}**
2. **{{name|Your date of birth}}**

*enough* needs your date of birth because every age in the forecast is counted from it: when you
can use your super, when the Age Pension starts, and more.

When both are filled in, press **{{name|Start my plan}}**.

### Nothing is saved until you press the button

*enough* doesn't save anything until you press **{{name|Start my plan}}**. It doesn't fill anything in for
you, and it doesn't make anything up. If you close it before pressing the button, nothing
happens.

### What comes next

After you press the button, the page opens. Now you can tell *enough* about your home, your super
and your pay. Part 2 shows you how, starting with [Chapter 8](#household-people).

If you live with a partner, you add them next, in the **{{name|Household and settings}}** box
(see [Chapter 8](#household-people)).

### If you've used *enough* before

You only see the welcome box when there's no plan on your computer yet. If you want to wipe
everything and begin again, see [Chapter 82](#starting-again).

### Pip and Jo

Pip started their plan by typing **Pip** and **1 January 1971**. Then Pip added Jo as the second
person.

## Chapter 8: Who lives here? {#household-people}

*enough* works for one person or for a couple. Each person has their own section in
**{{name|Household and settings}}**.

### The boxes for each person

- **{{name|Name}}**
- **{{name|Date of birth}}.** Every age in the forecast is counted from this.
- **{{name|Pay per fortnight, before tax (not take-home), today's money}}.** This is
  your pay for two weeks **before** tax comes out. Look on your payslip for the "gross" amount,
  not the amount that lands in your bank. Type **0** if you don't work. It's before tax because
  that's the number Centrelink checks to work out your Age Pension. This check is called the
  **income test** (see [Chapter 49](#means-test), which explains how it works). It's asked for per
  fortnight because that's how Centrelink measures pay. Money your investments earn doesn't belong
  in this box — that's counted separately (see [Chapter 49](#means-test)).
- **{{name|Work Bonus balance $}}.** Leave this at **0** unless you already get the Age Pension. ([Chapter 50](#work-bonus)
  explains it.)
- **{{name|Work Bonus last topped up on}}.** The day Centrelink last topped your Work Bonus balance up to
  $4,000. Leave it blank if it never has been. ([Chapter 50](#work-bonus) explains why it matters.)
- **{{name|Already had a super pension before this plan}}.** Choose **{{name|No}}** unless you've already been paid a pension
  from your super. Almost everybody says No.
- **{{name|Division 293 charge comes out of}}.** This is about an extra tax for people who earn a lot.
  Most people can leave it alone (see [Chapter 27](#division-293)).

### Adding your partner

If you live with a partner, press **{{name|Another person}}**. A second section appears for them.

### Your job pays into your super too

You don't need to type in the super your job pays for you. *enough* adds it by itself, based on
your pay.

### A top-up for lower earners

If you earn up to $45,000 a year, the government adds a little to your super for each year you
work. It's called the **low income super tax offset**. It gives back the 15% tax your super fund
takes from the money your job (and any salary sacrifice) puts into your super: that is, 15% of it,
at least $10 and at most $810 a year. It's paid after the financial year ends, so *enough* puts
it into your super in the July after. It doesn't count towards the limits on how much you can put
into super, and your fund takes no tax from it. Up to June 2027 the limits are lower: income up to
$37,000, and at most $500.

Two more rules decide it. At least a tenth of your income has to come from working, and your
income is counted the tax office's way, so a salary sacrifice counts as income and a loss on a
rented-out home doesn't bring it down. *enough* assumes you live in Australia for tax purposes.

*enough* only works it out for a whole financial year, so it never pays one for the part-year your
plan starts in. If all of your super has become a pension by the July it would be paid, *enough*
leaves it out and says so in that month's notes. The tax office would still pay it, so from then
on the forecast looks slightly worse than it should.

In Pip and Jo's plan, Jo's pay is low enough for it in each of the four years from July 2027 to
June 2031. Jo's last half-year of work, before they both retire in January 2034, would qualify too,
but Jo's super is a pension by then, so that one is left out.

### Your pay goes up

*enough* raises your pay a little every year, in line with how fast wages usually grow
(see [Chapter 15](#assumptions)). It keeps paying you until you add a change, like retiring ([Chapter 17](#retire-date)) or working
fewer hours ([Chapter 18](#part-time-work)).

Nothing is saved while you type. When you're finished, press
**{{name|Save the figures and this date together}}** ([Chapter 14](#as-at-date) explains the date).

## Chapter 9: Your super accounts {#super-accounts}

Each person can have one or more super accounts. Each account has its own little group of boxes.

### The boxes for each account

- **{{name|Super phase}}.** Is this jar still growing, or is it already paying you? Pick one:
  - **{{name|Still building up (accumulation)}}:** money goes in and nothing comes out. This is most
    people's super while they work.
  - **{{name|Paying you an income (account-based pension)}}:** you've retired and it pays you.
  - **{{name|Paying you while you still work (transition to retirement pension)}}:** it pays you a bit
    while you're still working (see [Chapter 25](#transition-to-retirement)).
- **{{name|Balance}}.** How much is in the account. Copy it from your latest super statement.
- **{{name|At retirement, 60 or older}}.** What happens to the account on the day you retire:
  - **{{name|Becomes a pension}}:** it starts paying you. This is what you'd usually pick.
  - **{{name|Stays as it is}}:** it keeps growing and doesn't pay you.

### What "Becomes a pension" means {#becomes-a-pension}

When you retire at 60 or older, *enough* turns your super into a pension. After that:

- the money it earns is no longer taxed
- it **must** pay you at least a small part every year, and the part gets bigger as you get older
- anything you don't spend goes into your savings

This only happens if you're 60 or older when you retire. If you retire younger, the account stays
as it is. A **transition to retirement pension** ([Chapter 25](#transition-to-retirement)) becomes a full,
ordinary pension at the same switch.

It also stays as it is if the account holds more than the **transfer balance cap**: the most that
the law lets you turn into a tax-free pension. Very few people have that much. *enough* doesn't
split an over-the-cap account between a pension and an accumulation account — it's all or nothing,
so the whole account keeps growing as accumulation instead.

This matters if the account is over the cap and its owner is younger than Age Pension age. In real
life, the part above the cap would become a pension. It would then be counted straight away in the
**assets test** — Centrelink's check of what you own, used to help work out the Age Pension (see
[Chapter 49](#means-test)). But *enough* instead treats the whole account as still building up. So
it won't be counted until Age Pension age, later than the real rules allow. **This makes the answer
look better than it really is** for a younger partner with an over-the-cap account.

It also stays as it is if you answer **Yes** to **{{name|Already had a super pension before this plan}}**
([Chapter 8](#household-people)). A pension you were paid before this plan starts has already used up
some of your transfer balance cap. *enough* has no way to know how much is left. So rather than
guess, it leaves this account as it is, and explains why in the month you retire. **No** is the
right answer for almost everybody.

### Watch out: switching to a pension can mean a SMALLER Age Pension

A pension counts in the assets test straight away, at any age. Money still building up in super
doesn't count until you reach Age Pension age. So imagine a younger partner switches their account
to a pension at retirement, before they reach Age Pension age. By Centrelink's rules, the household
can suddenly look like it owns more. Their Age Pension can then come out **lower**, not higher,
right after the switch.

### More than one account?

Press **{{name|Another super account}}** to add one. Most people have just one each.

|---|---|---|

| Balance | 500000 | 50000 |

## Chapter 10: The fees your super charges {#super-fees}

Super funds charge money to look after your super. The fees come out of your account, so they make
it grow more slowly. Over many years, that adds up.

### Where to find your fees

Look at your yearly super statement, or your fund's website. Fees usually come in two kinds:

- **A dollar amount each year**, like $52.
- **A percentage of your balance**, like 0.6%. The bigger your jar, the more you pay.

### The fee boxes

- **{{name|Admin. fee, dollars a year, in today's money}}.** The dollar amount.
- **{{name|Admin. fee,}} % a year.** A percentage fee for running the account.
- **{{name|Cap on that percentage fee, per year $ (optional)}}.** Some funds say "we'll never charge more
  than this much a year". If yours does, type it here.
- **{{name|Investment fee,}} % a year.** A percentage fee for investing your money.

### Watch out: percent, not decimal

In these fee boxes, type the percentage **just like your statement shows it**. For 0.6%, type
**0.6**. (This is different from the boxes in [Chapter 15](#assumptions), which use decimals.)

To help you, *enough* shows what the fee comes to in dollars, right under the box. If you type a
number over 3%, it warns you: **"{{name|That's over 3% a year — check you meant percent.}}"**

### Empty is not the same as zero

If a box is empty, *enough* thinks your fund doesn't charge that fee at all. It never guesses a fee
for you.

### When the fee comes out, and how it keeps up with prices

Each month, *enough* charges the fee **before** working out that month's growth — the same way a
real fund does. So the money taken as a fee never earns anything for you. The percentage fee (and
its cap) is worked out on that month's own balance, so it naturally rises and falls with your
balance by itself. The **dollar** fee and its cap are different: they're typed in today's money, and
*enough* raises them a little every year so they keep the same buying power. Before you retire, they
rise with wages. Once the account is paying you a pension, they rise with prices instead — which is
how real super funds, like Aware Super, actually raise them each year.

### Fees after you retire {#fees-after-retirement}

Some funds charge different fees once your super starts paying you. There's a second group of boxes
starting **{{name|After retirement}}…** for that. Leave them all empty and *enough* keeps charging the same
figures as before. But now they're raised with **prices**, not wages — that's the rule once your
super has started paying you as a pension (see [Chapter 9](#super-accounts)).

### Pip and Jo

| Box | Pip | Jo |
|---|---|---|
| Administration fee, dollars a year | 52 | 52 |
| Investment fee, % a year | 0.6 | 0.6 |
| What *enough* shows under the investment fee | = {{fig pip-investment-fee-a-year nearest-1|$3,000}} a year | = {{fig jo-investment-fee-a-year nearest-1|$300}} a year |

These fees are the reason Pip and Jo end up with less super than they would with no fees at all.

## Chapter 11: Insurance inside your super {#super-insurance}

Many super accounts come with insurance. It pays out money if something bad happens. The cost
(called a **premium**) comes out of your super.

### The three kinds

- **Death cover.** Pays your family if you die.
- **TPD cover** (total and permanent disability). Pays you if you get so sick or hurt that you can
  never work again.
- **Income protection.** Pays you part of your pay for a while if you can't work for a time.

### The boxes

They sit under the heading **{{name|Insurance in this account — $ a year, today's money, all optional}}**.
Every figure is dollars a year, in today's money, apart from the two ages. Every box can be left empty.

- **{{name|Death cover}}.** The death cover premium.
- **{{name|TPD cover}}.** The TPD cover premium.
- **{{name|Cover type}}.** Either **{{name|Fixed cover (the same amount every year)}}** or
  **{{name|Age-based cover (the fund's age scale)}}**. Your statement says which. Choose one only if you
  typed a premium.
- **{{name|Admin. fee}}:** The insurance administration fee.
- **{{name|Income protection}}.** The income protection premium.
- **{{name|Benefit period}}.** How long income protection would pay you: **{{name|Paid for up to 2 years}}**,
  **{{name|Paid for up to 5 years}}** or **{{name|Paid up to age 65}}**.
- **{{name|Death cover ends at age}}** and **{{name|TPD cover ends at age}}.** The age your fund stops
  each cover, from its insurance terms. Type a whole number from 16 to 70, or leave it empty for 70.

Leave everything empty if you have no insurance in your super.

### When the insurance stops

- Death cover and TPD cover each stop at the age you typed for them, or at 70 if you left the box
  empty. They also stop when your super becomes a pension. The age is set by your fund's terms,
  not by law. 70 is the latest *enough* accepts, because Aware Super's prices stop at 69. Many
  funds end TPD cover at 65, so check your fund's terms.
- Income protection stops at 65.
- The insurance administration fee is charged while any of the three covers is. When all of them
  have stopped, it stops too.

If you want to cancel or cut back your cover earlier, see [Chapter 56](#insurance-changes).

## Chapter 12: Your other investments, and why *enough* wants to know what you paid {#investment-parcels}

Some people own shares or managed funds **outside** super. You can buy and sell these whenever you
like. *enough* keeps track of them too.

This box is only for shares and managed funds. It isn't for your home or for any other property.

### One row for each purchase

Every time you bought some, that's one row. Press **{{name|Another investment — shares or managed funds}}**
to add a row. Each row has three boxes:

- **{{name|What you paid}}**
- **{{name|When you bought it}}**
- **{{name|What it's worth now}}**

If the date at the top of the section is after 1 July 2027, each row has a fourth box,
**{{name|Worth just before 1 July 2027}}**. Type what the holding was worth at the end of 30 June
2027: a statement from that day will show it. *enough* can't work it out itself, because your plan
starts after that day. If your plan starts on or before 1 July 2027 the box isn't shown, and *enough*
works the figure out as the forecast passes that day.

### Why *enough* wants to know what you paid

When you sell an investment for more than you paid, the extra is called a **gain**. You pay tax on
the gain. So *enough* needs to know what you paid to work out the tax.

It also needs to know **when** you bought it. If you've owned it for more than a year, only half
the gain is taxed. From 1 July 2027 that is true only of the part of the gain made before that day.
The part after it is taxed a different way. [Chapter 30](#capital-gains-and-franking) explains both.

**What you paid never changes.** It's the fixed figure the tax on a future sale is measured
against (the tax office calls it the **cost base**). Growth only ever adds to "What it's worth
now" — what you paid stays exactly as you typed it.

### Pip and Jo

Pip and Jo have no investments outside super.

**What-if:** In March 2020, Jo bought some shares for $10,000. Today they're worth $14,000. Jo
would type **10000**, the date in March 2020, and **14000**. If Jo sold them today, the gain would be $4,000.
Jo has owned them for more than a year, so only half of it ($2,000) would be taxed. That is the rule
for a sale today. For a sale after 1 July 2027, only the part of the gain made before that day would
be halved ([Chapter 30](#capital-gains-and-franking)).

**Where this comes from:** [CGT discount (ATO)](https://www.ato.gov.au/individuals-and-families/investments-and-assets/capital-gains-tax/cgt-discount), [Cost base of assets (ATO)](https://www.ato.gov.au/individuals-and-families/investments-and-assets/capital-gains-tax/calculating-your-cgt/cost-base-of-asset) and [Keeping records of shares and units (ATO)](https://www.ato.gov.au/individuals-and-families/investments-and-assets/capital-gains-tax/shares-and-similar-investments/keeping-records-of-shares-and-units). In the appendix: [Capital gains](#sources-capital-gains).

## Chapter 13: Your home loan and offset account {#home-loan-and-offset}

### Your home {#home-status}

The **{{name|Home}}** box asks whether you own the home you live in. Most people pick either
**{{name|Own it and live in it}}** or **{{name|Do not own a home}}**. (Other choices cover moving out or renting your home. See
Way 7 and Way 9.)

This box doesn't ask what your home is worth. For the Age Pension, your home doesn't count, so the
answer only changes which Age Pension limits apply to you.

**One choice is different.** Once you've been moved out for more than two years, the law says your
old home should now be counted as something you own, for the Age Pension. *enough* only has a value
for it if you've also added an "Enter aged care" change ([Chapter 52](#aged-care)). That's the only
place you tell *enough* what your home is worth. With no such change, there's no figure to put on
it, and *enough* won't invent one. So it leaves the home out of the **assets test** altogether in
that one case — Centrelink's check of what you own, used for the Age Pension (see
[Chapter 49](#means-test)). **This makes the answer look better than it really is**: in real life,
more of what you own would count towards the Age Pension than *enough* shows you.

### Your home loan {#home-loan}

Copy three numbers from your latest loan statement:

- **{{name|Amount still owed}}**
- **{{name|Interest rate a year (0.061 is 6.1%)}}.** It's a decimal: for 5%, type **0.05**.
- **{{name|Repayment each month}}**

They sit together under the heading **{{name|Home loan}}**.

Leave all three empty if you have no home loan.

Nothing is ever paid into the offset by the loan itself — the offset only ever reduces the
interest charged, as described below. The repayment stops the month the loan is fully repaid, and
that last repayment is only whatever was still owed, not a full one.

Owing money on your home doesn't change your Age Pension. Your home loan is never subtracted from
what else you own. Even while Pip and Jo still owe $250,000 on the house, their $10,000 offset
still counts in full.

### ⚠️ Don't count your loan twice

*enough* adds your loan repayment **on top of** your spending. So when you tell *enough* how much
you spend ([Chapter 20](#spending-plan)), **leave the loan out**. If you include it, *enough* will think you spend
more than you really do.

### Your offset account {#offset-account}

**{{name|Offset account balance (blank if you have none)}}** is your everyday savings. For *enough*, this is
the jar everything is paid into and out of.

If you have a home loan, the money in your offset makes the loan cheaper: you only pay interest on
what you owe **minus** what's in the offset.

For a couple, there's also a box for how much of the offset belongs to the first person. It starts
at 50%. It only affects whose income the interest is, for tax.

Each month, the Age Pension looks at the balance the forecast has actually reached by then — not
the figure you typed when you set the plan up. And unlike some other savings, an offset account
always counts in the assets test. There's no way for it to be left out.

### When the loan changes

If you'll get a new loan, a new rate or a new repayment later, don't change these boxes. Add a
change for that date instead (see Chapters [41](#loan-payoff-goal) to [43](#refinancing)).

## Chapter 14: The day your numbers are from {#as-at-date}

At the top of **Household and settings** is a box called **{{name|As at}}**. It holds one date.

### What the date means

The **{{name|As at}}** date is the "today" of your plan. It means: "These numbers were true **on this day**."

The forecast starts on this date. Its first month shows exactly what you typed, and growth starts
the month after.

### The numbers and the date are saved together

That's why the button says **{{name|Save the figures and this date together}}**. You can't save one without
the other.

### Why this matters

Imagine you read your super balance off today's statement, but the **{{name|As at}}** date says last year.
*enough* thinks that balance was true last year, and grows it for a whole extra year. Your super
looks bigger than it really is, and **nothing warns you**.

So every time you update your numbers, update the date too.

### Moving the date can change your plan

Things you've planned with dates ([Chapter 16](#dated-entries)) are measured against this "today":

- Move the date **forward past a one-off**, like a planned purchase, and *enough* treats it as
  already paid. It disappears from the forecast.
- Move the date **forward past a standing level**, like a spending level or a retirement. Then it
  applies from the very **start** of the forecast, instead of from its own date.
- Move the date **back past something that already happened**, and *enough* pays for it again.

*enough* doesn't warn you about this either. The forecast just changes. So be careful when you
move the date.

### Pip and Jo

Pip and Jo's numbers are **{{name|As at}}** 1 September 2026.

Nobody knows how fast prices, pay or investments will grow. So *enough* asks for your **guesses**.

These are **your** guesses, not the law. The government's own numbers are built into *enough* and
can't be changed here (see [Chapter 79](#rules-updates)).

### Decimals, not percentages

In these boxes, rates are **decimals**: type **0.025** for 2.5%. (The fee boxes in [Chapter 10](#super-fees) are
different. They use percentages.)

### The main boxes {#assumption-boxes}

| Box | What it means | Pip and Jo |
|---|---|---|
| **{{name|Planning horizon age}}** | How old the youngest person gets before the forecast stops | 100 |

**CPI** stands for the Consumer Price Index — the official measure of how fast the price of

The **{{name|Growth of super and investments a year}}** box changes the answer more than anything else. *enough* uses the same one for
every account, every year, compounded monthly. It's a good idea to try a lower guess and see what
happens.

This one rate does not touch everything, though: your offset account and your Work Bonus balance
earn nothing from it, and your home is never grown at all.

### The other boxes {#other-assumption-boxes}

- **{{name|Cash and offset interest}}.** What your cash and offset account earn a year, as a decimal.

- **{{name|Yearly payout, share of value}}** — what your investments pay you each year — and

- **{{name|Super income the fund is taxed on, share of balance}}** — how much of your super balance is
  treated as income each year — and **{{name|Share of that already company-taxed}}**, the company tax part of
  that. These are about the tax your super fund pays. Leave them empty if you're not sure.

  This is a special, lower rate your own fund uses — not the return above it. It's not a dollar
  amount. A dollar amount would matter less and less as your balance grows, making your plan look
  better than it really is.

  Once an account is paying you a pension, this share becomes zero. Any company tax already paid on

- **{{name|Pensioners’ cost of living, yearly rise (PBLCI)}}** and **{{name|Age Pension wage benchmark, yearly rise (MTAWE)}}.**
  Two more guesses about prices and wages that change how the Age Pension grows. The first is how

  them alone.

  and wages. From there, the benchmark grows using your MTAWE guess.

  The real, published rates run to 19 March 2027. Every date after that is a forecast from these two
  guesses — unless your plan states its own dated figures, which always win instead.
- **{{name|Contingency buffer — kept back, in today's money (blank if you have none)}}.** Money you want to

## Chapter 16: Every change has a date {#dated-entries}

There are two kinds of information in *enough*:

1. **What you have today.** Your pay, super, loan and so on. This goes in
   **{{name|Household and settings}}** (Chapters [8](#household-people) to [15](#assumptions)), and you can change it any time.
2. **Things that will happen later.** Retiring, spending more or less, selling the house. These go
   in a box called **{{name|Change the plan}}**.

### A change is an entry with a date

Each thing that will happen later is called an **entry**. Every entry has a date. On that date,
*enough* makes it happen in the forecast.

For example, Pip and Jo's plan has an entry that says "Both stop working, 1 January 2034".

### Nothing is ever erased

Here's something surprising: **you can't delete an entry, and you can't edit one.** Once you add
it, it stays.

Why? So you can always see how your plan has changed over time, and nothing disappears by accident.

### Switching an entry off

Instead of deleting, you **switch it off**. Press **{{name|Switch off}}** next to the entry. It stays in your
plan, but *enough* ignores it.

Changed your mind? Press **{{name|Switch back on}}**.

Switching off only affects the plan (scenario) you're looking at. Other scenarios keep using it
(see [Chapter 38](#scenarios)).

### Oops! Fixing a mistake

Typed the wrong number? It's two steps:

1. **{{name|Switch off}}** the wrong entry.
2. **{{name|Add entry}}** with the right number.

### Finding switched-off entries

Switched-off entries are hidden to keep things tidy. Tick **{{name|Show switched off}}** to see them again.

### Pip and Jo

**What-if:** Pip adds an entry to spend $80,000 a year, but meant {{fig spend-from-now nearest-1|$70,000}}. Pip switches off the
$80,000 entry and adds a new one for {{fig spend-from-now nearest-1|$70,000}}. The $80,000 one is still there, switched off, if Pip
ever wants to look back.

## Chapter 17: Picking a day to retire {#retire-date}

Telling *enough* you're retiring is one entry, added in the **{{name|Change the plan}}** box.

### Adding it

Pick **{{name|Retire}}** from the **{{name|Type}}** list, then fill in:

- **{{name|From}}** — the date you stop work.
- **{{name|Owner}}** — **{{name|Household}}** (both of you, on the same day) or one of your names, if only one of
  you is stopping.
- **{{name|Stopping for good, or cutting down?}}** — pick one:
  - **{{name|Stopping work for good}}** — the usual answer. From that date, the person it names is paid no
    more salary in the forecast. There is nothing else to fill in. If they're 60 or older, a line under
    the choice says the forecast starts an account-based pension on that date, and that you need to ask
    your fund to do it: the fund won't do it by itself. If they're under 60, the line says stopping
    doesn't let them take super early.
  - **{{name|Cutting down, with a transition to retirement (TTR) pension}}** — for one person, aged 60 to 64 on
    the date, who keeps working (on fewer days or the same) and draws a transition to retirement pension
    ([Chapter 25](#transition-to-retirement)). Fill in **{{name|Days a week}}** and the salary those days pay.
    This isn't retiring, so it adds a **{{name|Change work hours and pay}}** entry, not a Retire entry.
    If no pension is running yet, two more boxes appear, and the same answer also starts one on that
    date ([Starting one from a later date](#ttr-start)): pick the **{{name|Which super account pays the pension}}**,
    type the **{{name|Amount to move $}}** into it and, if you like, the **{{name|Share taken out each year (%)}}**.
    If the person is under 60 or 65 or older on the date, or already stops work for good on or before it,
    the form says why and adds nothing. So it does if the amount is empty and they have only one super
    account still building up.

### What it doesn't do by itself

Retiring is only about pay stopping. Two things some people expect it to do, it doesn't:

- **It doesn't touch your super account's phase on its own — except at 60 or older.** If you're 60
  or older on the day you retire, any super account you still have "still building up" switches
  automatically into an income stream called an **account-based pension**. It then starts paying you
  at least a legal minimum each year. It doesn't switch if you retire before 60, if the account is
  already very large, or if you choose to keep it as it is.
- **It doesn't spend anything for you.** What you spend after you retire is a separate fact — see
  [Chapter 20](#spending-plan).

Retiring isn't forever, either. If you go back to work later, add a change of work hours after the
Retire entry ([Chapter 18](#back-to-work)).

### Stopping work at 60 or older, and before 60

The law says stopping a job at 60 or older counts as "retiring" for super, full stop. Nothing else
has to be true, so *enough* treats a Retire entry dated on or after your 60th birthday as retiring.

Stopping before 60 is different. It only counts as retiring from 60 if your super fund is satisfied
that you never mean to work again. *enough* never asks about that, so it doesn't guess. Retire
before 60 and *enough* counts your super as locked until you turn 65.

### If somebody's pay never stops

*enough* checks this for you. If nobody's Retire entry ever reaches a person by the end of the
forecast, it says so plainly on the screen — something like *"Pip is still paid a salary when the
plan ends."* That way, a missing Retire entry never hides quietly inside a rosy forecast. The same
line appears if someone goes back to work after retiring and never stops again.

### Pip and Jo

Pip and Jo's real plan has one Retire entry: **{{name|Household}}**, dated **1 January 2034**. It stops both
incomes on the same day, exactly as [Chapter 2](#pip-and-jo) describes.

**What-if:** What if Pip wanted to keep working a year longer than Jo? Switch off the household
entry ([Chapter 16](#dated-entries) shows how) and add two of your own instead — one naming Pip, dated later, and one
naming Jo, dated as before. Switching an entry off never erases it. So you can always switch
the household one back on, remove the two you added, and Pip and Jo are exactly as they were.

## Chapter 18: Working a little less before you stop {#part-time-work}

Some people don't stop working all at once — they cut back their hours for a while first.
*enough* has an entry for exactly that: **{{name|Change work hours and pay}}**.

### Adding it

- **{{name|From}}** — the date the new hours start.
- **{{name|Owner}}** — who is changing hours.
- **{{name|Days a week}}** — how many days they now work.
- **{{name|Salary a year, in today's money $}}** — what those days pay, for the whole year.

From this date, that figure replaces the pay on file. *enough* raises it every year with wage
growth, the same way it raises ordinary pay, until another change — or a Retire entry — replaces it.

### Stopping altogether this way

Type **0** days and **0** salary together, and the salary stops from that date — the same effect,
for pay, as retiring. But it is **not** the same as a Retire entry: it doesn't switch a super
account into an account-based pension the way turning 60-or-older and retiring does ([Chapter 17](#retire-date)).
If you mean "this person has actually retired", use a Retire entry instead.

### What it needs

The two boxes go together: any number of days other than nought needs a salary to go with it, and a
salary of nought needs nought days. Leaving either blank is refused rather than guessed.

### Going back to work after you retire {#back-to-work}

Retiring isn't a one-way door. If someone goes back to work after their Retire entry, add a
**{{name|Change work hours and pay}}** entry for them dated **after** the Retire date, with the days and the
salary. From that date the salary is paid again, and everything that follows from pay follows too:
income tax, the employer's super, and the Age Pension income test.

What happens to super:

- **Super you had when you retired stays yours to use** if you retired at 60 or older. Going back
  to work doesn't lock it up again.
- **That amount is fixed in dollars.** It is what each account held when you stopped, less anything
  you took out before going back. It doesn't grow: the earnings on it after you stopped are locked,
  like new money. Take some out and there is that much less left to take. If an account falls below
  it, through fees or a bad year, what you can take falls with it and doesn't come back.
- **An account-based pension keeps paying.** Work doesn't stop it.
- **New money is locked.** Your employer's super goes into an account still building up. It stays
  locked, with all the earnings, until you stop work again at 60 or older, or turn 65. Then all of
  it is yours to use.
- **Asking for more than is free gets you what is free.** A lump sum bigger than that fixed amount
  pays the fixed amount and no more; the rest stays in the account.
- **A pension account can't take new money.** If every account of yours became a pension when you
  retired, your employer's super has nowhere to go, and the forecast stops with a message saying so.
  A real fund would open a new account for you; *enough* can't open one part-way through the plan.
  To show it, give that person a second super account with a nil balance, set to stay as it is at
  retirement, and say which account the employer pays into.

To stop again, add another **{{name|Retire}}** entry after the return. Pay stops on that date and the new
money is freed if you are 60 or older. Nothing becomes a pension a second time, unless the first time
you stopped was before 60 (which freed nothing): then this later stop is the one that turns your
super into a pension. The one other exception is a transition to retirement pension started after
going back (below).

**A transition to retirement pension after going back.** Once you are back at work you can start one
again ([Chapter 25](#ttr-start)), dated on or after the day you went back. It has to come from an
account that stayed as it was when you stopped, not one that became a pension then. Answering
**{{name|Cutting down, with a transition to retirement (TTR) pension}}** on a Retire entry dated after
the break counts as going back by itself. The next time you stop work, that pension becomes a full
pension, and nothing else changes at that stop. It first has to fit under the transfer balance cap
together with what already became a pension; if it doesn't, it stays as it is until 65. If you don't
stop again before 65, it becomes a full pension on your 65th birthday.

A change dated on the Retire day itself, or before it, doesn't count as going back: the Retire entry
wins. So does **0** days. A return in the same month as the Retire is refused; date it in a later
month. The **{{name|Life stages}}** strip ([Chapter 64](#life-stages)) shows the person as Working again,
with a label such as *"Back at work 2030 · 61"*.

**Where this comes from:** [Superannuation Industry (Supervision) Regulations, reg 6.01(7)](https://www.ato.gov.au/law/view/document?DocID=REG%2F19940057%2F6.01%287%29) and [Superannuation Industry (Supervision) Regulations, Schedule 1](https://www.ato.gov.au/law/view/document?DocID=REG%2F19940057%2FSch1). In the appendix: [Super and contributions](#sources-super).

### Pip and Jo

Pip works full time and Jo works part time ([Chapter 2](#pip-and-jo)). Neither has a change of hours on file today
— both go from their current pay straight to retiring on 1 January 2034.

**What-if:** What if, two years before they retire, Jo dropped to three days a week? Add
**{{name|Change work hours and pay}}**, owner Jo, dated **1 January 2032**, **{{name|Days a week}}** 3, and a **{{name|Salary a year}}**
that reflects the smaller pay those three days bring — in today's money. From that date, Jo's pay in
the forecast falls to match, right up to 1 January 2034, when the existing Retire entry for the
household still stops it altogether.

## Chapter 19: Seeing the day your money runs out, and how to make it later {#shortfall}

Some plans work for a while and then don't. *enough* doesn't hide that — it shows you the exact
month, and lets you try changes until it doesn't happen at all.

### Where you see it

On the big wealth picture ([Chapter 62](#wealth-charts)), a plan that runs short of money carries a marker labelled
**{{name|Shortfall Age}}** — the first month the forecast asks for money it doesn't have. And in the income
picture ([Chapter 66](#income-chart)), you'll read a sentence such as *"From [that month] this plan asks for money it
does not have."* If your money never runs out, you won't see either. Instead you'll read that your
own savings and super cover everything, right through to the last month the forecast covers. That
also means you never reach a month where the Age Pension is the only money you have left — so the
chart never shows a *Pension-Only Age* marker for their plan.

### Pip and Jo

Ask *enough* this question of Pip and Jo's real plan, and the answer is good news: **their money is
never short**. No month, all the way to {{fig last-month month|January 2073}} when Jo turns 100, asks for more than they
have.

### What-if: making a shortfall, and then fixing it

**What-if:** Suppose Pip and Jo decide to spend about 40% more once they stop working, on top of
the plan in [Chapter 83](#pip-and-jo-facts). Ask *enough*, and a Shortfall Age appears. The money runs short from around
**{{fig shortfall-spending-40-percent-more month|May 2051}}**, when Pip is 80 and Jo is 78 — and at that higher spending, it never recovers.

Two ways to push that day back, tried one at a time:

- **Work one year longer.** Retiring in January 2035 instead of January 2034, with everything else
  the same, pushes the shortfall all the way out to around **{{fig shortfall-spending-40-percent-more-retire-2035 month|September 2067}}** — Pip 96, Jo 94.
- **Work two years longer.** Retiring in January 2036 removes the shortfall altogether — the money
  is never short again.

Spending less ([Chapter 20](#spending-plan)), or checking how much is actually safe to spend ([Chapter 22](#sustainable-spend)), are the
other two levers most households reach for. None of this changes Pip and Jo's real facts. Switch
the what-if entries off, and their own plan — which was never short to begin with — is exactly as it
was.

## Chapter 20: Your spending plan: money for fun, food and holidays {#spending-plan}

Your everyday spending — food, bills, insurance, running a car, holidays, the lot — is told to
*enough* with a **{{name|Spending change}}** entry.

### Adding it

- **{{name|From}}** — the date this level of spending starts.
- **{{name|Owner}}** — usually **{{name|Household}}**.
- **{{name|Annual household spending in today's money, NOT counting home loan repayments $}}**.

That label means exactly what it says twice over. First, it's **in today's money**. Type what that
standard of living costs right now, and *enough* raises it a little every year in line with the
price rises you've assumed ([Chapter 5](#real-and-pretend-money)) — so you go on affording the same life, rather than the same
number of dollars. Second, it **excludes your home loan repayment**. If you have a home loan on
file, *enough* works out the repayment separately and adds it on top of this figure. Most people
think of their spending with the mortgage already included. That's exactly why the box says so:
typing a figure that already includes the loan would count it twice — and make the forecast show you
running out of money years before you really would.

### It's a standing figure

From this entry's date, a twelfth of it is charged every month, until another Spending change
entry replaces it. A plan can carry several — one for now, and others for later years, each with
its own date — and *enough* will use whichever is current in any given month.

One-off costs, like a new car or a big trip, don't belong in this figure at all: each of those is
its own entry, covered next in [Chapter 21](#one-off-spend).

### Not sure what to type? What other retirees spend

If you don't know what figure to type, a good place to start is the **ASFA Retirement Standard**. ASFA
(the Association of Superannuation Funds of Australia) is the group that speaks for super funds. It
works out what two kinds of retired life cost, and updates the figures every three months:

- **Modest** — a bit better than living on the Age Pension alone: the basics, a cheap car, not much
  eating out, holidays close to home.
- **Comfortable** — good health insurance, a decent car, eating out, and a trip overseas now and
  then.

For the three months to June 2026, for people aged 65 to 84 who own their home outright, ASFA's figures were:

| | Modest | Comfortable |
|---|---|---|
| One person | $36,548 a year | $56,166 a year |
| A couple | $52,690 a year | $78,998 a year |

People aged 85 and over spend a little less: ASFA's comfortable figure for them is $53,964 for one
person and $74,484 for a couple. Its modest figures for people who rent are higher: $51,418 for one
person and $69,376 for a couple.

These figures fit the box well. They're in today's money, and they assume there's no home loan
left, so they already leave out the repayment. They're only a yardstick, though. Your own spending
is a better guide than anyone else's. The latest figures are on
[ASFA's website](https://www.superannuation.asn.au/consumers/retirement-standard/).

### Pip and Jo

Pip and Jo's real plan has several spending levels, each dated ([Chapter 83](#pip-and-jo-facts)): **{{fig spend-from-now nearest-1|$70,000}}** a year from
today, rising to {{fig spend-from-67 nearest-1|$75,000}} once the house loan is paid off at 67. Then it steps down as they get
older — {{fig spend-from-70 nearest-1|$70,000}} at 70, {{fig spend-from-75 nearest-1|$60,000}} at 75, {{fig spend-from-80 nearest-1|$55,000}} at 80, and {{fig spend-from-85 nearest-1|$50,000}} a year from 85. None of these
figures include their mortgage repayment, which is worked out separately from the loan details in
their plan ([Chapter 13](#home-loan-and-offset)).

**What-if:** What if they decided to spend more in the early retired years and less later, instead
of the steadier path above? Add a new Spending change dated at the year you mean, and the level in
force at that date takes over from then on — you don't need to touch the ones either side of it.

## Chapter 21: Big one-off buys, like a new car or a trip {#one-off-spend}

A new car, a roof, a wedding, a big overseas trip — these don't belong in your everyday spending
figure ([Chapter 20](#spending-plan)). *enough* has its own entry for a single cost, in its own month:
**{{name|One-off expense}}**.

### Adding it

- **{{name|From}}** — the month it happens.
- **{{name|Owner}}** — usually **{{name|Household}}**.
- **{{name|Amount, as it will cost on the day $}}**.

That's the whole of it — one box, one date. Notice the label says the opposite of [Chapter 20](#spending-plan)'s:
this figure is **not** in today's money. Type the actual price you expect to pay, on the day you
expect to pay it. *enough* doesn't raise it with inflation the way it raises your standing spending
figure — because you've already priced it for the day it happens, there's nothing left for the
forecast to add.

### One entry, one cost

Each big buy is its own entry. A car this year and a roof in five years' time are two separate
One-off expense entries, each with its own date and its own amount — not one figure covering both.

### Pip and Jo

Pip and Jo's real plan ([Chapter 83](#pip-and-jo-facts)) has no one-off expenses on file — every cost in their plan is
part of the standing spending figures in [Chapter 20](#spending-plan).

**What-if:** What if, in 2035, they bought a $15,000 car? Add a **{{name|One-off expense}}**, owner
**{{name|Household}}**, dated **1 June 2035**, amount **$15,000** — priced as it will actually cost that day,
not in today's dollars. *enough* charges that $15,000 once, in June 2035, on top of whatever their
standing spending figure is that month, and touches nothing else in their plan.

## Chapter 22: Asking *enough* how much is safe to spend each year {#sustainable-spend}

Rather than guessing at a spending figure and checking whether it works, you can ask *enough* to
work the sustainable figure out for you.

### The button

Where you see the **{{name|Financial Situation}}** sentences ([Chapter 60](#financial-situation)), press
**{{name|Work out what I could spend}}**. *enough* tries your whole plan at different spending levels, keeping the *shape* of it.
If you have several spending levels on file ([Chapter 20](#spending-plan)), every one of them is scaled by the
same amount — never flattened into a single number. Then it looks for the highest level where every
month is still paid for.

### What you're told

You'll always get one of a few kinds of answer:

- **Comfortable, with room to spare** — a figure above what you told it, and by how much.
- **Tight** — a figure below what you told it, and the gap.
- **Very comfortable** — funded at four times what you stated, which is as far as the search goes;
  the real ceiling is somewhere above that, unstated.
- **Nothing works** — even at a hundredth of your figure, some month still can't be paid for, so
  spending isn't what's driving the trouble; something else in the plan needs to change.
- **Nothing to test** — your plan doesn't say what you spend yet ([Chapter 20](#spending-plan) first).

Whatever the answer, *enough* also tells you three more things. Whether it assumed the Age Pension
keeps rising with wages — the law's own rule, and the single biggest assumption behind the figure.
Whether you've set a **contingency buffer**, money you never want to dip under ([Chapter 15](#assumptions)). And
whether your figure is apart from a home loan still being repaid.

### Pip and Jo

Ask *enough* this of Pip and Jo's real plan. The answer: **you could spend about {{fig sustainable-spend nearest-1|$80,932}} a
year, in today's money, and still pay for every month of this forecast — {{fig sustainable-spend-rise-percent nearest-1|16%}} more than the {{fig spend-from-now nearest-1|$70,000}}
a year your plan states.** Spend more than that, and {{fig sustainable-spend-first-short-month month|April 2058}} — Pip 87, Jo 85 — is the first month
the plan would ask for money it doesn't have. The answer keeps the shape of their plan: all seven of
their spending levels move up together, by the same {{fig sustainable-spend-rise-percent nearest-1|16%}}, not just the first one. They haven't set a
**contingency buffer**, so this tests only that every month is covered — nothing is held back on top of
that.

**Where this comes from:** [How pensions are indexed (DVA)](https://www.dva.gov.au/access-benefits/pensions-and-payments/ready-reckoners/indexation-of-income-support-pensions-and-allowances) and [Social security indexation (DSS)](https://www.dss.gov.au/income-support-payments/social-security-indexation). In the appendix: [The Age Pension](#sources-age-pension).

## Chapter 23: Putting extra in from your pay (salary sacrifice), and stopping later {#salary-sacrifice}

Asking your employer to put extra of your pay into super, before tax, is called **salary
sacrifice**. *enough*'s entry for it is **{{name|Salary sacrifice into super}}**.

### Starting one

- **{{name|From}}** — when the arrangement starts.
- **{{name|Owner}}** — whose pay it comes out of.
- **{{name|What this entry does}}** — choose **{{name|Sacrifice this much a year}}**.
- **{{name|Salary sacrificed a year, before tax, and the same number of dollars every year $}}**.
- **{{name|Super account}}** — which of that person's accounts it lands in, counted from 1.

It's a **standing** arrangement: it runs from this date until you add another entry that changes or
stops it. It isn't in today's money — it's the same number of dollars every year, since it's a
before-tax pay decision rather than a standard of living.

### Stopping it

Add another **{{name|Salary sacrifice into super}}** entry, choose **{{name|Stop sacrificing, from this date}}**
instead, and give the date it ends. What's already gone in stays in; your pay is simply taxed in
full again from there. This is different from switching the original entry off ([Chapter 16](#dated-entries)), which
removes it from every year it ever ran.

### What the forecast does with it

The fund takes its usual 15% out of what's sacrificed. Your employer's own compulsory super payments —
called the **Superannuation Guarantee** — count towards the same yearly limit as your sacrifice. So a large
sacrifice on top of a decent salary can be refused outright if the two together are over the year's
cap, currently $32,500, plus any cap left unused in the five years before — but only if your total
super was under $500,000 on the 30 June before the year. Being under the cap isn't proof you have
room to spare: unused cap from a year before your plan starts counts only if you type it in (see
below), so without it *enough* can refuse a catch-up contribution you're actually allowed to make;
and personal contributions you claim a deduction for aren't held at all. If your combined income and contributions are over $250,000, an extra tax applies — see
[Chapter 27](#division-293). And more than your pay can never be sacrificed: the excess is simply dropped, so the
arrangement forecast is never bigger than what you actually earn. Sacrificing never shrinks the
Superannuation Guarantee itself — it's always worked out on your salary **before** the sacrifice
comes off it. And it can only land in an account that's still building up, not one already paying
you a pension.

### Unused cap from earlier years, and the catch-up button {#catch-up-contributions}

**What unused room is.** Each year there's a limit on how much can go into your super before tax —
your employer's compulsory payments plus any salary sacrifice. If you put in less than the limit,
the part you didn't use isn't lost straight away: it stays available for the next five years. In a
later year you can put in more than that year's limit, and the extra uses up your oldest unused
amount first. After five years an unused amount expires.

**The $500,000 test.** You can only use unused amounts in a year if your total super — every account
you have — was under $500,000 on the 30 June just before that year. At $500,000 or more, the unused
amounts are still there and still counting down, but you can't use them that year. The $500,000
doesn't go up with prices, so more people cross it as balances grow.

**What the big table shows.** The **{{name|Unused super cap}}** column in the year-by-year table
([Chapter 65](#years-and-months)) shows, for each person, what's left of the year's limit once the
forecast's own contributions are counted, and when the oldest unused amount expires. "Closed" means
the $500,000 test shut it that year, or that *enough* doesn't know the 30 June balance (below). These
figures are in each year's own dollars, even when the table shows today's money, because the limits
are set in dollars by law.

**Telling *enough* about years before your plan.** *enough* works out unused room itself for every
whole year it forecasts. For the five years before your plan starts it can't know, so it counts them
as fully used unless you tell it. In **Household and settings**, each person has
**{{name|Unused concessional cap from myGov}}** boxes, one for each of those five years. To find the
figures, sign in to myGov, open the Australian Taxation Office, then go to **Super**, then
**Information**, then **Carry-forward concessional contributions**. It lists the unused amount for
each year. Leave a year blank if you don't know it — blank counts as used, which can only make the
forecast more careful.

If your plan's date isn't 1 July, *enough* also asks for each person's **total super balance on
30 June** just before the plan's first year. Your plan's balances are from a later date, so they
can't answer the $500,000 test for that year. myGov shows that 30 June figure on the same page. If
you leave it blank, *enough* treats the first year's unused room as closed.

**What the catch-up button does.** Where a year has unused room carried forward and open, the
table offers **{{name|Add a catch-up}}**. Pressing it adds two entries to the scenario you're looking
at: a salary sacrifice starting 1 July of that year and a stop on the next 1 July. The amount is the
room left after the forecast's own contributions for that year, rounded down to whole dollars, so
the year stays inside its limit. It's checked before anything is saved. If that person's pay
can't carry it, or they already have a salary sacrifice running that year, nothing is saved and
the message at the top of the page says why.

**What it doesn't do.** It's an offer, not advice, and nothing happens unless you press it. It
doesn't arrange anything with your employer or fund — you'd need to do that yourself. It isn't a
personal contribution you claim a deduction for; *enough* doesn't model those. And it isn't always a
win: if your income plus contributions are over $250,000, using unused room means more of your
contributions are hit by the extra 15% tax ([Chapter 27](#division-293)). You can switch the two
entries off like any other ([Chapter 16](#dated-entries)).

### The Guarantee started counting on 20 September 2026

Before that date, only what you sacrificed counted against the cap. From 20 September 2026, the law
counts your employer's Superannuation Guarantee too — so an arrangement that fit yesterday can be
refused today. For example: sacrificing $27,000 against a $150,000 salary used to be checked against
the $27,000 alone. Now it's checked as roughly $27,000 plus about $18,000 of Guarantee — around
$45,000 — against the $32,500 cap, and refused. From July 2027, only $25,000 of the cap is
guaranteed by law — the rest isn't set yet. So from then, if your sacrifice plus your Guarantee
add up to more than $25,000, *enough* refuses the plan by name instead of guessing.

### Pip and Jo

Neither Pip nor Jo has a salary sacrifice arrangement in their real facts ([Chapter 83](#pip-and-jo-facts)).

**What-if:** What if Pip sacrificed $15,000 a year into their super account from today? Add
**{{name|Salary sacrifice into super}}**, owner Pip, **{{name|Sacrifice this much a year}}**, amount $15,000, super
account 1. From today, Pip's take-home pay falls and their super account receives the sacrificed
amount less the fund's 15%, alongside the usual employer contributions — until you add a "Stop
sacrificing" entry or switch this one off.

## Chapter 24: Putting money into your partner's super {#spouse-contribution}

Adding some of your own after-tax money into your partner's super is a different entry from salary
sacrifice: **{{name|Put money into your partner's super}}**.

### Adding it

- **{{name|From}}** — the date, and it happens once.
- **{{name|Owner}}** — who is paying (the money always lands in the *other* person's account).
- **{{name|Amount, paid once, from your own after-tax money $}}**.
- **{{name|Your spouse's super account}}** — which of *their* accounts, counted from 1, and it must be one
  still being paid into rather than one already paying an income.

### What's different about it

This is your own money, already taxed. It's not a before-tax arrangement, so the fund takes no 15%
out of it, and it doesn't count towards the concessional cap that limits salary sacrifice ([Chapter 23](#salary-sacrifice)).

It can also cut up to $540 off your own tax bill — a **tax offset**, an amount taken straight off
what you owe. Whether you get this depends on your spouse's income for the *whole* financial year —
and *enough* can't know that yet on the day you add the entry. So it works the offset out only
after that financial year ends, not straight away.

A household with only one person in it has nobody to make this entry for — it needs a partner.

### Pip and Jo

**What-if:** What if Pip put $3,000 of their own savings into Jo's super this year, to help even up

## Chapter 25: Getting paid from super while still working (transition to retirement), and changing how much {#transition-to-retirement}

A **transition to retirement pension** — TTR for short — lets you draw an income from super while
you're still working, once you've reached your **preservation age**: the youngest
age you can ever take money out of super, which is 60. One you already have is a setting on
the super account. One you're planning to start later is an entry on the timeline.

### One you already have

In **{{name|Household and settings}}**, next to the super account that pays it, choose
**{{name|Paying you while you still work (transition to retirement pension)}}** from the phase list, then fill in:

- **{{name|Start date}}** — the day it started.
- **{{name|Share taken out each year (%)}}** — between 4% and 10% of the balance. Leave it empty and *enough* pays the legal
  minimum, the least the fund must pay you each year.

### Starting one from a later date {#ttr-start}

Use the **{{name|Start a transition to retirement pension}}** entry, or answer **{{name|Cutting down, with a transition to retirement (TTR) pension}}**
when you add a Retire entry ([Chapter 17](#retire-date)). Fill in:

- **{{name|From}}** — the day it starts. You must be 60 to 64 on that day.
- **{{name|Owner}}** — whose pension it is.
- **{{name|Super account}}** — which one, counted from 1.
- **{{name|Amount to move $}}** — how much to move into the pension. Leave it empty if you have set up
  a second account for it yourself (see below).
- **{{name|Share taken out each year (%)}}** — between 4% and 10%, or leave it empty for the legal minimum.

**The pension gets its own account.** While you keep working, your employer keeps paying into your
super, and a pension account can't take that money. So a fund starts the pension in a new account and
moves part of your super into it. Type that amount in **{{name|Amount to move $}}** and *enough* does
the same: on the first of the month the pension starts, it opens a new account and moves the amount
into it out of the account you named. That account stays as it is and your employer's money goes on
landing in it. If the account holds less than the amount by then, all of it moves. In the forecast the
new account is called "the TTR from super account 1" (or whichever account you named).

The new account is charged the same fees as the account the money came from, so you pay a second
administration fee. Most funds do charge one, but check yours: if it doesn't, the forecast is a little
worse than it will be. It has no insurance: the cover stays on the old account.

**Setting up the second account yourself still works.** Instead of an amount, add a second super
account now under **{{name|Household and settings}}**, holding the amount you plan to move, and name
it here with the amount left empty. If you are paid a salary, *enough* also has to know which of the
two accounts your employer pays into. Once you have two accounts still building up, a
**{{name|Employer pays into}}** box appears under your super accounts: pick the account your employer
pays into, and the other one can start the pension. Until you pick, the page won't save the second
account. This way you can give the second account its own fees and insurance.

**Until the start date, nothing changes.** The account is still "building up": if you're younger
than Age Pension age, it doesn't count for the Age Pension. From the start date it pays you, it
takes no more contributions, and it counts for the Age Pension (as its balance, and as deemed
income) whatever your age. Its earnings are taxed as before.

**It becomes a full pension** on the day you stop work for good (a later Retire entry), or on your
65th birthday, whichever comes first. The form refuses a start before 60, on or after 65, before the
plan's start date, or while you have stopped work: on or after your own Retire entry, until you go
back.

**After going back to work** ([Chapter 18](#back-to-work)) you can start one again. It becomes a full
pension the next time you stop, or on your 65th birthday, whichever comes first, and that stop changes
only this account. It has to come from an account that stayed as it was when you stopped (with an
amount, the money has to come from one): one that became a pension then can't start another, and the
form says so.

**One case works differently.** A pension started *before* you first stop, from an account you set to
stay as it is at retirement, is not turned into a full pension by that stop, or by any later one. It
runs until your 65th birthday.

### Changing the rate later {#ttr-rate-change}

Once it's running, use the **{{name|Change how much a transition to retirement pension pays}}** entry to
change the rate from a later date:

- **{{name|Super account}}** — which one, counted from 1.
- **{{name|New share taken out each year (%)}}** — again between 4% and 10%.

For a pension started with an amount to move, name the account the money came from: the change goes
to the pension's new account.

A mid-year change resets the payments for the rest of that year. But the year's total still can't
go above 10% of the balance on 1 July, or below the legal minimum. The change has to come before
the account converts to a full pension. That happens automatically at 65 at the latest — a full
pension has no rate left to change.

### Pip and Jo

Neither Pip nor Jo has a transition to retirement pension in their real facts ([Chapter 83](#pip-and-jo-facts)).

**What-if:** What if, from age 60, Pip drew a TTR pension from their super at 7% a year, alongside
still working? Add a **{{name|Start a transition to retirement pension}}** entry for Pip, dated Pip's
60th birthday, naming Pip's super account, with the amount Pip would move into the pension and 7% taken
out each year. Later, if Pip wanted to draw less, add a **{{name|Change how much a transition to retirement pension pays}}**
entry naming the same account and a new rate, dated from whenever the change should start: it changes
the pension's new account.

**Where this comes from:** [Transition to retirement income streams (ATO)](https://www.ato.gov.au/individuals-and-families/super-for-individuals-and-families/self-managed-super-funds-smsf/paying-smsf-benefits/income-stream-pension-rules-and-payments/transition-to-retirement-income-streams), [Superannuation Industry (Supervision) Regulations, reg 6.01(2)](https://www.ato.gov.au/law/view/document?DocID=REG%2F19940057%2F6.01%282%29) and [Pension payment rules (ATO)](https://www.ato.gov.au/individuals-and-families/super-for-individuals-and-families/self-managed-super-funds-smsf/paying-smsf-benefits/income-stream-pension-rules-and-payments). In the appendix: [Super and contributions](#sources-super).

## Chapter 26: Taking a lump sum out of super {#super-lump-sum}

Withdrawing a single amount from super, rather than an ongoing income, is the
**{{name|Take a lump sum from super}}** entry.

### Adding it

- **{{name|From}}** — the date of the withdrawal.
- **{{name|Owner}}** — whose super it comes from.
- **{{name|Amount $}}**.
- **{{name|Super account}}** — which one, counted from 1.

### The age rule, said plainly

**This only works from age 60.** A withdrawal before 60 is refused outright, not estimated. The tax
on an early withdrawal depends on how much of your super is "taxable" versus "tax-free" money — a
split *enough* doesn't track. So it won't guess at the number; it simply refuses. From 60, the
forecast shows **no tax** on the withdrawal, which is right for most ordinary super funds. It
assumes a "taxed" fund, though. An older government or public-sector scheme that never had tax
taken on the way in is still taxed even after 60 — and this forecast would be too optimistic about
one of those. A withdrawal bigger than what's in the account simply pays out whatever is there.

*enough* also won't let you withdraw from an account that's already paying you a full pension
income. Nor can you withdraw from an account whose benefit is still locked away below your own
**preservation age**, the youngest age you can ever take money out of super (60). Even then, you
also have to stop working or turn 65 to take it out freely.

### Pip and Jo

Neither Pip nor Jo has taken a lump sum from super in their real facts.

**What-if:** What if, at 63 when they retire, Pip withdrew $20,000 from their super as a lump sum
towards a home renovation? Add **{{name|Take a lump sum from super}}**, owner Pip, dated their 63rd birthday
or later, amount $20,000, super account 1. Because Pip is over 60, the forecast shows this
withdrawal with no tax.

## Chapter 27: An extra tax for bigger earners (Division 293) {#division-293}

If your income and your before-tax super contributions together are large, an extra tax called
**Division 293** applies to the low-taxed part of those contributions.

### How it's charged

Once combined income and contributions are above **$250,000**, an extra 15% is charged — on top of
the fund's ordinary 15% — on the lesser of your before-tax contributions and however much you're
over that $250,000 line. *enough* works this out and charges it monthly, straight out of cash.

### Choosing which pocket pays it {#division-293-election}

By law, you can instead ask for the charge to be released out of one of your own super accounts,
rather than paid from your own cash. *enough* lets you make that choice in
**{{name|Household and settings}}**, one selection per person:

- **{{name|Division 293 charge comes out of}}** — either
  **"{{name|No election (Division 293 charge paid from own cash)}}"**, or one named accumulation account.

**"{{name|No election}}" is a real, deliberate answer** — it's what the law does if you decide nothing, and
*enough* never picks an account on your behalf, even if you only have one. Only accumulation
accounts are offered; an account already paying you a pension can't be named.

Which pocket pays matters for more than tax. While you're under Age Pension age, money sitting in
super isn't counted when Centrelink works out your Age Pension ([Chapter 49](#means-test)), but money
sitting in cash is. So electing to pay the charge from super, rather than cash, can leave you with a
higher Age Pension in the years before 67, if you're affected by this tax.

In the law, making this choice means formally asking the Tax Office to release the money from your
fund. That's also why only accounts still building up are offered. Asking to release money from an
account already paying you a pension would count as cashing part of that pension out early — and
*enough* doesn't model that.

### Pip and Jo

At their real incomes, {{fig pip-pay-a-year nearest-1|$91,000}} and {{fig jo-pay-a-year nearest-1|$39,000}} a year ([Chapter 83](#pip-and-jo-facts)), neither Pip nor Jo comes close to
the $250,000 threshold, so Division 293 has no effect on their plan.

**What-if:** What if Pip's pay, plus a large salary sacrifice ([Chapter 23](#salary-sacrifice)), pushed their combined
figure over $250,000 for a year? *enough* would charge the extra 15% that year. If Pip made no
election in **{{name|Household and settings}}**, it would come out of Pip's cash. Naming a super account
there instead would take it from super, leaving more cash — and, if Pip is under 67, potentially a
higher Age Pension for the household.

**Where this comes from:** [Income Tax Assessment Act 1997, s 293-20](https://www.ato.gov.au/law/view/document?DocID=PAC%2F19970038%2F293-20), [The Act that sets the 15% rate](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2013A00087/latest/text) and [Division 293 tax (ATO)](https://www.ato.gov.au/individuals-and-families/super-for-individuals-and-families/super/growing-and-keeping-track-of-your-super/caps-limits-and-tax-on-super-contributions/division-293-tax-on-concessional-contributions-by-high-income-earners). ## Chapter 30: Tax when you sell, and the franking credits bonus {#capital-gains-and-franking}

Selling an investment outside super, and the yearly payouts it makes along the way, both have tax
effects *enough* works out for you.

### Tax when you sell

When you sell a parcel, the gain — what it sold for, less what it cost — is taxed as ordinary
income in the year you sell it. Hold a parcel for **more than 12 months** before selling, and only
**half** the gain is taxed: the well-known "CGT discount" (capital gains tax discount). Which parcel
is treated as sold first ([Chapter 29](#buy-sell-investments)'s **{{name|Which to sell first}}** box) can change how much of the gain
gets that discount, because each parcel has its own purchase date and its own cost.

From 1 July 2027 the law changes, and *enough* follows it. A parcel you hold on that day is treated
as sold and bought back at its value just before that day. The gain up to then still gets the half-gain rule
when you sell. The gain after it doesn't: its cost is raised with inflation instead, and all of what's
left is taxed. There's also a 30% minimum tax on the gain after 1 July 2027: if the ordinary tax on it
comes to less than 30%, you pay the difference, rounded down to the dollar. You don't pay the minimum
tax for a year in which you're paid the Age Pension at any time. If the pension starts part way
through the year, tax already charged on a sale that year comes back when the year is settled in July.

The inflation used to raise the cost is the rate on the **{{name|Forecast assumptions}}** screen
([Chapter 15](#assumptions)), because the real figures for future years aren't published yet. When
the minimum tax is part of a tax line, the ⓘ beside that line says so.

### The yearly payout, and the franking credits bonus {#investment-payout}

Shares and managed funds usually pay their owners something each year, on top of going up or down in
value. Two boxes on the **{{name|Forecast assumptions}}** screen ([Chapter 15](#assumptions)) tell *enough* about this:

- **{{name|Yearly payout, share of value}}**: what your investments pay you each year (0.04 means 4% of what
  they're worth) — leave it blank if they pay you nothing. It's asked for as a **share of value**,
  not a fixed dollar figure. A fixed dollar figure would fall behind as your holding grows — showing
  less income, and less tax, than you'd really owe. Typing a nil here is refused, so it can't be
  confused with leaving the box blank.
- **{{name|Share already company-taxed}}**: how much of that already has company tax paid on it (0.7 means 70%) — leave it blank if
  you're not sure.

Once a year, on the last day of the financial year, *enough* works out that payout and adds it to
your tax for the year, alongside any gain you've realised. The payout isn't handed to you as cash —
it's reinvested straight back into what you hold, raising its cost base. So it doesn't change what
you can spend day to day, but it still adds to that year's tax bill. If part of the payout already
had company tax paid on it — a **franking credit** — that credit is applied at the same year-end
settlement. Anything left over after your tax bill is refunded to you in full. Leave the second box
empty and *enough* treats the whole payout as ordinary, unfranked income. A figure typed there
without a payout rate above it is refused, because there's nothing yet for it to be a share of.

### Pip and Jo

Pip and Jo have no non-super investments today, so neither of these boxes has anything to act on for
them yet. If they added shares in [Chapter 29](#buy-sell-investments)'s what-if, filling in a payout rate and a franked share
on the **{{name|Forecast assumptions}}** screen would bring both effects into their forecast from the next
30 June onward.

**Where this comes from:** for the tax when you sell, [Income Tax Assessment Act 1997, Division 115](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A05138/latest/text) and [CGT discount (ATO)](https://www.ato.gov.au/individuals-and-families/investments-and-assets/capital-gains-tax/cgt-discount); for franking credits, [Income Tax Assessment Act 1997, s 207-20](https://www.ato.gov.au/law/view/document?DocID=PAC%2F19970038%2F207-20), [s 67-25](https://www.ato.gov.au/law/view/document?DocID=PAC%2F19970038%2F67-25) and [Refund of franking credits for individuals (ATO)](https://www.ato.gov.au/individuals-and-families/investments-and-assets/shares-funds-and-trusts/investing-in-shares/refund-of-franking-credits-for-individuals). In the appendix: [Capital gains](#sources-capital-gains) and [Franking credits](#sources-franking).

|---|---|
| **{{name|Date it happened}}** | The day of the purchase |
| **{{name|Which way}}** | **{{name|Money out (a spend)}}** or **{{name|Money in (a refund)}}** |
| **{{name|Amount, in dollars}}** | e.g. `45` or `45.50` |
| **{{name|Description}}** | What it was, e.g. "Woolworths" |
| **{{name|Account desc.}}** | Which account it came from, e.g. "Everyday account" |

Press **{{name|Add this transaction}}** and it's recorded straight away, under the form, newest first.

### Why bother?

Writing down real spending lets *enough* work out an honest yearly spending figure from what you
actually buy, instead of a guess. Chapters [35](#categories) to [37](#derived-spending-plan) show how: putting spending into boxes, letting
rules guess the boxes for you, and turning the total into your spending plan.

### What this is not

- **It doesn't connect to your bank.** You type each purchase in by hand, or bring in a whole bank
  list at once ([Chapter 34](#csv-import)).
- **It doesn't change your forecast by itself.** Recording a transaction is just writing it down. Your
  spending plan only changes if you later choose to use what you've recorded ([Chapter 37](#derived-spending-plan)).
- **Once recorded, a transaction is permanent.** There is no edit box and no delete button anywhere on
  this screen, and no form is ever pre-filled from something you already wrote down. A mistake is
  fixed by *reversing* it — [Chapter 33](#reversing-a-transaction) shows how.

### Pip and Jo

Pip picks up milk and bread on the way home and types it in that evening: **12 September 2026**,
**{{name|Money out}}**, **$14.50**, "Woolworths", "Everyday account". It sits at the top of the list until Jo
adds the next one.

## Chapter 33: Undoing a mistake: reversing a transaction {#reversing-a-transaction}

Typed $500 when you meant $50? There's no way to edit it. Instead, next to the transaction is a button
called **{{name|Reverse…}}**.

### How it works

1. Press **{{name|Reverse…}}**. Nothing is written yet — it just asks you to confirm.
2. *enough* shows what it's about to do and asks again: **{{name|Yes — reverse it}}** or
   **{{name|Cancel — leave it as it is}}**.
3. Pressing **{{name|Yes — reverse it}}** adds a brand new transaction, dated the same day, for the exact
   opposite amount. The $500 spend and its $500 refund now cancel each other out.

Both transactions stay in your history forever — the original and the one that cancels it. Nothing is
deleted, so you can always see what happened, including the mistake.

### One transaction can only be reversed once

Once a transaction has been reversed, the **{{name|Reverse…}}** button disappears from it, and from the
reversal itself. There is nothing to reverse twice, and reversing a reversal would just be the
original transaction all over again.

### Then add the correct one

Reversing only cancels the wrong entry — it doesn't type the right one for you. After reversing the
$500, add a fresh transaction for the real $50.

### Pip and Jo

Jo typed $500 for a $50 pharmacy visit. Jo presses **{{name|Reverse…}}**, confirms with **{{name|Yes — reverse it}}**,
and a new $500 refund appears, dated the same day as the mistake. Jo then adds a proper transaction:
$50 out, "Chemist", same date. All three now sit in the history, and the $500 error nets to nothing.

## Chapter 34: Bringing in your bank list all at once (CSV import) {#csv-import}

Typing every purchase by hand is slow. Most banks let you export your transactions as a file — usually
with a name ending in **.csv**. The **{{name|Import a bank CSV file}}** box reads that file for you.

### What happens

The file is read **in your own browser** and never sent anywhere except into your own transaction
history on your own computer.

The first time *enough* sees a file laid out a particular way, it asks a few questions so it can
understand it:

- **{{name|The first row holds column names}}** — tick this if your bank's file has headings like "Date" and
  "Amount".
- Which column is the date, and **which way the date is written** (day/month/year and so on).
- Which column is the description.
- Whether the amount is one column (negative for money out) or two separate columns for money out and
  money in.
- **{{name|Record these against the account}}** — a name for which account this file came from.

*enough* remembers the answer. The next time you bring in a file with the same column names — in any
order — it reads it straight away and asks nothing.

### Nothing is missed, and nothing is doubled up

Before anything is added, you're shown a preview of the rows it can read, and a list of any rows it
couldn't. Each one comes with its line number and the reason, so nothing vanishes silently. Only when you press
**{{name|Import}}** (it says how many rows, e.g. "Import 42 rows" or "Import the 40 good rows" if a couple
can't be read) does anything get written.

Importing the same file twice adds nothing the second time: a row already recorded is recognised by
its date, amount, description and account, and skipped. Two genuinely identical purchases on the same
day both still come through the first time.

### Pip and Jo

Pip exports last month's list from the bank's website and imports it. The first time, *enough* asks
which column is which; from then on, that bank's files are read without asking again.

## Chapter 35: Putting spending into boxes (categories) {#categories}

Every transaction can be given a **{{name|Category}}** — a box that says what kind of spending it was.

### The four boxes

| Category | What it means | Counted as spending? |
|---|---|---|
| **{{name|Committed}}** | Bills and things you have to pay — rent, insurance, loan repayments | Yes |
| **{{name|Discretionary}}** | Everyday spending you choose — food, fun, going out | Yes |
| **{{name|One-off}}** | A big, unusual buy that won't happen every year | Shown, but not counted |
| **{{name|Other (not spending: left out of the spending figure)}}** | Not a spend at all — money moving between your own accounts, paying off a card, buying an investment | No |

### How to set one

Next to a transaction, choose a **{{name|Category}}** and, if you like, type a **{{name|Subcat. (opt.)}}** — a
free-text label like "Groceries" or "Streaming". *enough* offers subcategories you've used before
under that Category, so you don't have to remember your own spelling. Press **{{name|Save category}}**.

### Categories are never overwritten

Choosing a new Category for a transaction doesn't erase the old one — it adds a new record on top.
The most recent one is the one shown and used everywhere else. So if you get it wrong, just choose
again; nothing is lost.

### Pip and Jo

Pip categorises last week's Woolworths shop as **{{name|Discretionary}}**, subcategory "Groceries", and the
loan repayment as **{{name|Committed}}**. A one-off carpet clean goes in as **{{name|One-off}}**, so it won't inflate
next year's estimate.

## Chapter 36: Letting *enough* guess the boxes for you (rules) {#categorisation-rules}

Categorising every transaction by hand gets old fast. A **rule** watches for words in a description
and suggests a Category — but it never categorises anything without you saying yes.

### Making a rule

Fill in:

- **{{name|Description contains}}** — e.g. "WOOLWORTHS" (capitals don't matter).
- **{{name|Only for the account (leave empty for any)}}** — optional, if you only want it to match one account.
- **{{name|Suggest the category}}** and, optionally, **{{name|Subcategory (optional)}}**.

Press **{{name|Save rule}}**. Nothing is categorised yet. Saving a rule with the same words and the same
account again replaces the old one from then on.

### Reviewing what it matches

Every transaction the rule matches is shown together, as one card: for example, *"23 transactions
match "WOOLWORTHS" → Discretionary / Groceries"*. You review the whole batch at once:

- **{{name|Accept all}}** categorises every transaction listed — each can still be recategorised by hand
  afterwards.
- **{{name|Reject}}** writes nothing at all. The batch just won't be shown again until you reload the page or
  what it matches changes — it isn't remembered as "rejected".

### If two rules both match

Where two of your rules match the same transaction, the newer rule wins. The card says so — for
example, that some matches were taken from an older rule of yours. A transaction you categorised by
hand yourself is never shown in any batch again.

### Turning a rule off

Press **{{name|Retire}}** on a rule to stop it suggesting for good. Whatever it already categorised stays as it
is — retiring a rule only stops future suggestions.

### Pip and Jo

Pip saves a rule: "WOOLWORTHS" → Discretionary / Groceries. Every past and future Woolworths
transaction turns up in one batch to accept, instead of being set one at a time.

## Chapter 37: Turning real spending into your spending plan {#derived-spending-plan}

Once you've recorded and categorised a stretch of real transactions, *enough* can work out what you
actually spend — and offer to use that figure in your plan.

### Working it out

Press **{{name|Work out my spending from transactions}}**. *enough* looks at your most recent stretch of
history — up to the last 365 days, ending on your latest recorded transaction (at least 28 days of
history is needed, or it says so instead of guessing) — and tells you, in plain sentences:

- what you spent a year, all up, in today's money (scaled up to a full year if you have less than a
  year of history);
- how much of that was Committed and how much Discretionary;
- anything **not categorised** is counted in, so nothing gets left out of the figure by accident — but
  it's worth categorising, to check;
- **{{name|One-off}}** spending is shown but never included — add anything you expect again as its own
  one-off expense instead ([Chapter 21](#one-off-spend));
- **{{name|Other}}** — transfers and the like — is shown and never included either.

**{{name|Show which transactions}}** opens the exact list this figure is built from, so you can check it.

### Nothing is applied until you say so

This figure is only ever **offered**. Nothing is added to your plan unless you press
**{{name|Use this as my spending from today}}**, which adds one spending change to the scenario you're looking
at, dated your plan's "as at" day. Any later spending change you already have still applies from its
own date. **{{name|Cancel — keep my spending as it is}}** writes nothing.

Working this out is never automatic — not when the page loads, not after an import. Any change you
make to your transactions clears the offer on screen. So you always have to ask again before using a
figure — and it's always based on your transactions as they now stand.

### Pip and Jo

After a year of recording and categorising, Pip and Jo press
**{{name|Work out my spending from transactions}}** and see they've actually spent close to their planned {{fig spend-from-now nearest-1|$70,000}} a year. They press
**{{name|Use this as my spending from today}}** to replace their guess with the real figure.

## Chapter 38: Making a pretend future (*Scenarios*) {#scenarios}

A **Scenario** is one version of your future. Every plan starts with one, but you can make more, each
its own "what if".

### The Scenarios box

Each Scenario appears as a row with:

- a radio button, titled
  **"{{name|Showing this scenario — the chart, the table, and where a new change is added}}"** — pick one to look at it, and any new change you add next goes into that one;
- its **name**, in an editable box, with a **{{name|Rename}}** button beside it;
- **{{name|Clone}}** and **{{name|Delete}}** buttons ([Chapter 39](#copying-a-scenario) covers Clone);
- what it **inherits**, if anything;
- its **{{name|Shortfall Age}}** — whether, and when, this Scenario's money runs out.

Your pick is saved, so *enough* opens on the same Scenario next time.

### Starting a brand new one

Type a name and press **{{name|New scenario}}**. A new Scenario like this starts completely bare: no changes of
its own and nothing inherited — just your Household and Settings, exactly as they stand (Part 2). It's
the honest starting point for "what if none of my plan's changes had happened at all".

To build a "what if" on top of what you already have, don't start bare — clone an existing Scenario
instead, which [Chapter 39](#copying-a-scenario) explains.

### Deleting one

**{{name|Delete}}** removes the Scenario itself, never any of the changes it listed — those stay in your plan
for any other Scenario that also uses them (see "Every change has a date", [Chapter 16](#dated-entries), for what a
change actually is). *enough* won't delete a Scenario that another Scenario is built on top of. And
it won't delete your last Scenario — a plan always needs at least one.

### Pip and Jo

Pip and Jo's whole plan lives in one Scenario, called **{{name|Baseline}}**. Everything in [Chapter 2](#pip-and-jo)'s telling
of their plan is Baseline's.

## Chapter 39: Copying a plan and changing just one thing {#copying-a-scenario}

Want to try a change without losing your existing plan? Press **{{name|Clone}}** on a Scenario's row.

### What cloning does

A clone is a new Scenario that starts by inheriting **everything** the one you cloned has — every
change in it — and adds **nothing** of its own yet. From there, add just the one change you want to
try, and only your clone has it.

### The surprise worth knowing

Cloning doesn't copy anything — it inherits it **live**. If you later add a new change to the original
Scenario, every Scenario cloned from it picks that new change up too, automatically, from that day on.
What's yours alone in a clone is only the changes you add directly to it. Turning a change off in a
clone only switches it off there, in that one Scenario — it stays on everywhere else.

### Why you can't delete a Scenario others are built on

Because a clone depends on its original staying put, *enough* won't let you delete a Scenario while
any clone still inherits from it. You'd have to delete or rebuild the clones first. A plain
Scenario with nothing built on it, and nothing it inherits from, can always be deleted on its own
([Chapter 38](#scenarios)).

### Pip and Jo

Pip and Jo clone **{{name|Baseline}}** and call the copy "What if Pip works one more year". In the clone, they
add one change: Pip's retirement pushed back by a year. Everything else — the move, the payoff, the
spending steps — is still there, inherited from Baseline, exactly as before. Baseline itself is
untouched, and because the clone now depends on it, Baseline can't be deleted while the clone exists.

## Chapter 40: Putting plans side by side on one picture {#comparing-scenarios}

With more than one Scenario, you can draw them on the same chart, side by side.

### Switching to Compare

Above the chart, under **{{name|Chart view}}**, choose **{{name|Compare — ticked scenarios}}** instead of
**{{name|Focused — means-test split, active scenario}}**. A **{{name|Scenarios shown}}** box appears where you tick
which ones to draw.

### What the lines mean

Each line is one Scenario's total wealth, so you can see straight away which one runs out first, and
which lasts longer. A toggle lets you switch every line to **{{name|What Centrelink counts}}** instead.
This shows each Scenario's assets that count for the Age Pension test ([Chapter 49](#means-test)), measured
against the two cut-off amounts — useful if you want to compare what the test sees, not total wealth.

### What Compare doesn't show

The Compare chart draws one plain line per Scenario. It never shows the split between protected and
counted money that you see in Focused view — with several Scenarios on one chart, that split would
draw every dollar twice over. Switch back to Focused and pick a single Scenario to see that split for
it.

Each Scenario's **{{name|Shortfall Age}}** is listed as text beside the Scenarios box, not written on the
chart itself. With several Scenarios compared at once, that many captions on one chart would sit on
top of each other and be unreadable.

### Trying changes with sliders {#try-changes}

Under the chart, open **{{name|Try changes}}**. It shows up to three sliders for the Scenario you have
picked:

- **Retirement**: moves the retirement date, one month at a time, from next month up to age 75. For a
  date the two of you share, it shows both your ages on that date.
- **Spending**: makes every spending level in the plan bigger or smaller together, from 50% to 150%.
  It shows what you would spend in the first year.
- **Refinance loan amount**: changes how much the new loan is for, from $0 up to twice the amount.

A slider only moves a change that is already in the plan. If there is none, it is greyed out and says
why. To add one, use **{{name|Change the plan}}**.

As you move a slider, the chart draws just two lines: your Scenario and **{{name|Sandbox (unsaved)}}**.
Beside the sliders, four answers are shown for each: when the money runs out, when you would live on
the Age Pension alone, when the loan is paid off, and how much you could spend. The last one takes a
second to work out, so it says "updating…" until it is ready.

Some mixes can't be forecast, such as stopping work before a transition to retirement pension starts.
Then the sandbox line and its answers go blank, and a sentence says why.

Nothing is kept until you press **{{name|Save as a scenario}}** and give it a name. The new Scenario
builds on the one you started from, so later changes to that one still reach it. **{{name|Discard}}**
puts every slider back. If you pick another Scenario or leave the page with sliders moved, *enough*
asks first.

### Pip and Jo

Pip and Jo tick both **{{name|Baseline}}** and their "What if Pip works one more year" clone. Two lines appear,
and the Shortfall Age text beside each Scenario shows straight away whether the extra year of work
changes when — or whether — their money runs out.

## Chapter 41: Setting a goal date to pay off the loan {#loan-payoff-goal}

Want to check your retirement date against a date you have in mind for the loan — without actually
changing anything yet? Add a **{{name|Set a home loan payoff or offset target date}}** change.

### What it asks for

Just a date, and an optional **{{name|Note}}** to say what the date means to you — "loan paid off" or "offset
covers what's owing", say. There's no amount box: a target date doesn't move any money at all.

### What it tells you

For each person in your household, it says plainly whether their own retirement date, as your plan
currently stands, falls **before** or **after** the target. If money is still owing on the loan at the
end of the target month, it says exactly how much. It also notes that adding this target changed
nothing about the loan itself — so if you actually want it cleared by then, you'll need a real
payoff ([Chapter 42](#early-loan-payoff)).

This is a fact-check, not a verdict. It never says the target is "met" or "missed" — because with
two people retiring on two different dates, one might be before the target and the other after, and
neither is more right than the other.

### Pip and Jo

Pip and Jo could add a target date of **1 July 2038** — the day after they plan to pay the loan off —
to check it against their own retirement in January 2034. The sentence for each of them would simply
say their retirement falls before that date.

## Chapter 42: Paying the loan off early {#early-loan-payoff}

Ready to pay the whole loan off, rather than just aim for a date? Add a **{{name|Home loan — pay off in full}}**
change, dated the day you want it gone.

### What it asks for

Nothing about the amount. There's no box for it, because the amount isn't yours to state — it's
whatever is still owed on that exact date, plus that month's interest, worked out by the forecast
itself. It's paid the same way any other big cost is: first from cash, then investments, then super,
whichever your plan has to spend.

If there's no loan actually running on the date you choose — say, it's already been paid off, or a new
one hasn't started yet — this change is refused. There would be nothing to pay off.

### What it doesn't do

This entry only pays off the **whole** loan. Paying off part of it with a lump sum isn't something
*enough* models at all ([Chapter 43](#refinancing) says more about what a loan change can and can't do).

### Why there are two entries for the loan, not one

You might expect to pay a loan off by setting its balance to zero on the "Home loan — set or change
the terms" change instead ([Chapter 43](#refinancing)). *enough* refuses that: a balance of $0 there would be a payout
with no money ever found for it. This entry is the one that actually finds the money, from wherever
your plan has it.

### Pip and Jo

Pip and Jo's own plan includes exactly this change, dated **30 June 2038** — the day they use the rest
of their savings to clear the whole loan, at 67 and 65. From 1 July 2038 nothing is owing on it.

## Chapter 43: Getting a new loan (refinancing) {#refinancing}

Whether you're changing lenders, changing the rate, or taking a loan out for the first time, it's the
same change: **{{name|Home loan — set or change the terms}}**.

### The three boxes

| Box | What it means |
|---|---|
| **{{name|Amount still owed, from this date $}}** | The new balance |
| **{{name|Interest rate a year, as a decimal (0.061 is 6.1%)}}** | The new rate |
| **{{name|Repayment each month $}}** | What you'll pay each month from now on |

All three are always required. And all three are always what the loan becomes from that date —
nothing from before is carried over, even if only one figure has actually changed. State all three
every time, even the ones that stay the same.

This same entry is also how you tell *enough* about a loan you take out for the first time, or a fresh
one after an earlier loan has already been paid off.

Typing **$0** as the amount still owed is refused here — this entry sets the loan's terms, and a
$0 balance would really be a payout that no money was ever found for. To pay the loan off, use
[Chapter 42](#early-loan-payoff)'s "Home loan — pay off in full" entry instead.

### What it doesn't model

Paying part of the loan off with a lump sum, redrawing money back out, making extra repayments, and
interest-only periods aren't modelled by this entry, or anywhere else in *enough*. To clear the whole
loan, use [Chapter 42](#early-loan-payoff)'s entry instead.

### One refusal worth knowing

The repayment has to be enough to cover the month's interest. Whether it does depends on your offset
balance at the time — and *enough* only knows that once it runs the whole forecast. If the repayment
turns out not to be enough, the **whole forecast** is refused, and the message names this change and
the month.

### Pip and Jo

Pip and Jo's own plan uses this entry, dated **1 April 2027**, to record their move: the loan becomes
**{{fig loan-after-move nearest-1|$350,000}}**, still at **5%** a year, still **{{fig repayment-after-move nearest-1|$2,300}}** a month. Worth knowing: *enough* records the
move itself only as this change in the loan. Their plan has no separate "sold this house, bought that
one" entries behind it — a new loan is the whole of the fact this build needs.

## Chapter 44: Selling the house {#selling-the-home}

To record selling your home outright, add a **{{name|Sell the home}}** change.

### The boxes

- **{{name|Sale price (your own estimate), in today's money $}}** — required.
- **{{name|Selling costs (optional), in today's money $}}** — agent's fees and the like.
- **{{name|Of that, set aside for a new home, in today's money $}}** — optional, if you plan to buy again.

### What happens to the money

Whatever is still owed on your home loan, and on any Home Equity Access Scheme loan ([Chapter 51](#home-equity-access)), is
paid off first out of the sale. This is capped at what the sale actually raises, so you're never
asked to find more than it covers. What's left lands as ordinary cash. Selling your own home is never taxed:
your main home has always been exempt from Capital Gains Tax.

### What it means for the Age Pension

From the sale date, your household is treated as a **non-homeowner**, which uses higher asset
thresholds for the Age Pension — unless you set part of the proceeds aside for a new home. That
set-aside amount is left out of the **assets test** — the check on what you own, used to work out
your Age Pension ([Chapter 49](#means-test)) — for up to **24 months** from the sale. Your household
keeps being treated as a homeowner for that whole time. It's still counted for the **income test** —
the check on what you're paid — though, just at the lower of the two assumed earning rates
Centrelink uses ([Chapter 49](#means-test) explains this "deeming"). This window doesn't stretch past
24 months, even in special circumstances. After that, the set-aside money counts in full and your
household becomes a non-homeowner, exactly as if nothing had been set aside.

A household can only sell its home once in a plan, and only if it has one to sell.

### Pip and Jo

Selling outright is a genuine what-if for Pip and Jo, because their real plan never actually sells
their home — their April 2027 move ([Chapter 43](#refinancing)) is recorded only as a change to the loan. As a what-if,
they could try: what if, instead, they actually sold their first home, set aside enough for the new
one, and bought it ([Chapter 45](#buying-a-home))?

**Where this comes from:** for the tax side, [Your main residence (ATO)](https://www.ato.gov.au/individuals-and-families/investments-and-assets/capital-gains-tax/property-and-capital-gains-tax/your-main-residence-home) and [Income Tax Assessment Act 1997, Subdivision 118-B](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A05138/latest/text); for the Age Pension side, [Assets test for Age Pension (Services Australia)](https://www.servicesaustralia.gov.au/assets-test-for-age-pension) and [Guide to Social Security Law 4.6.3.90](https://guides.dss.gov.au/social-security-guide/4/6/3/90). In the appendix: [Capital gains](#sources-capital-gains) and [Your home, and living apart](#sources-home).

## Chapter 45: Buying another home after selling {#buying-a-home}

After selling, add a **{{name|Buy a new home}}** change to record moving into the next one.

### The boxes

- **{{name|Purchase price (your own estimate), in today's money $}}** — required.
- **{{name|Stamp duty and fees (optional), in today's money $}}**.

### How it's paid

The price and costs are paid first out of whatever you set aside from the sale ([Chapter 44](#selling-the-home)). Anything
still needed beyond that is paid the way any other large cost is — from pay, pensions, cash,
investments and, last, super you're able to take out. If all of that together still can't cover it,
the purchase is refused rather than showing money your household doesn't have.

### The rules

- This must settle in a **later month** than the sale it follows.
- It only works after a **{{name|Sell the home}}** change in the same scenario — *enough* doesn't model buying
  a first home, buying twice, or buying while someone is in aged care.
- From the settlement date, the new home is your exempt main residence again, and anything left of the
  set-aside simply becomes ordinary cash.
- There's no box to say who the new home belongs to. A home, like the loan against it, is a fact
  about the whole household, not one person — so this change doesn't ask.

### Pip and Jo

Continuing the what-if from [Chapter 44](#selling-the-home), Pip and Jo could add a **{{name|Buy a new home}}** change a month or
two after their pretend sale, to see how the numbers compare with the loan-change version of their
move they actually plan to make.

**Where this comes from:** for the tax side, [Your main residence (ATO)](https://www.ato.gov.au/individuals-and-families/investments-and-assets/capital-gains-tax/property-and-capital-gains-tax/your-main-residence-home). In the appendix: [Capital gains](#sources-capital-gains).

## Chapter 46: Putting house-sale money into super (the downsizer contribution) {#downsizer-contribution}

After selling, each of you can put some of the proceeds straight into super — outside the usual yearly
limits. Add a **{{name|Downsizer contribution to super}}** change to record it.

### The boxes

- **{{name|Amount from the home sale, in today's money $}}**.
- **{{name|Your super account}}** — which of your own accounts it goes into.
- **{{name|Did you or your spouse own the home for 10 years or more?}}** — Yes or No.

### The rules, in plain words

- Up to **$300,000** each, and together no more than the sale price. Unlike most figures in
  *enough*, this limit doesn't rise with inflation — it's the one figure in this entry that stays
  $300,000 for good.
- Each of you must be **55 or older** — including whoever wasn't on the title.
- The home must have been owned for **10 years or more**.
- It must be paid within **90 days** of the sale.
- Each person can only do this **once**.

The fund takes no tax out of it, it doesn't count against either of the usual super contribution
limits, and it can't be claimed as a tax deduction. It goes into an ordinary, still-growing super
account, not a pension account — and once it's in, it counts for the Age Pension the same as any other
super. It's paid from your sale set-aside first, then like any other large cost, on [Chapter 45](#buying-a-home)'s
pattern. If it can't be paid in full, it's refused.

### Pip and Jo

This only makes sense alongside an actual sale — so it belongs with the what-if in Chapters [44](#selling-the-home) and [45](#buying-a-home),
not with Pip and Jo's real plan, which never sells the house. As part of that what-if, either of them
could add up to $300,000 of the sale proceeds straight into their own super.

## Chapter 47: Renting the house out {#renting-out-the-home}

Moving out but keeping the house? Add a **{{name|Rent out the home}}** change.

### The boxes

- **{{name|Weekly rent (your own estimate), in today's money $}}** — required.
- **{{name|Council rates a year (optional), in today's money $}}**.
- **{{name|Insurance a year (optional), in today's money $}}**.
- **{{name|Agent's fees a year (optional), in today's money $}}**.
- **{{name|Repairs a year (optional), in today's money $}}**.
- **{{name|Tenancy ends on this date (optional)}}**.

All the money boxes are in today's prices. Unlike a sale price, though, the rent and each cost keep
rising with inflation for as long as the tenancy runs — not just once when you add the change.

### What renting out means for tax and the pension

The rent, less those costs, counts as taxable income — the same as any rental. So if the costs are
more than the rent, that loss can reduce your other income for tax. For the Age Pension
**income test** — the check on what you're paid, used to work out your Age Pension
([Chapter 49](#means-test)) — and for the aged care means test, the net rent is counted in full. It's
never deemed ([Chapter 49](#means-test) explains "deeming"), and here a loss can't reduce your other
income — it's simply treated as zero.

Renting the **whole** home out is treated as deciding not to move back. Unless someone has already
left it for aged care, there's no 12-month grace period — from this date, it counts as a
non-homeowner asset for the Age Pension. *enough* only models renting the whole home out, not a
single room while you still live there, and two tenancies can't overlap.

A rented-out home needs a value to be assessed at, and this entry has no box for one. *enough* uses
the home value from an "Enter aged care" change ([Chapter 52](#aged-care)) instead — so renting the
home out is refused unless one of you has an aged-care entry stating what the home is worth.

### One refusal worth knowing

Selling the home more than **6 years** after you started renting it out is refused. Beyond six years,
the tax office's main-residence exemption only applies in part — and this build doesn't work out a
partial exemption.

As a what-if, Pip and Jo could try renting their home out for a few years instead of paying it off —

## Chapter 48: The Age Pension: money for older people {#age-pension}

[Chapter 3](#retiring) mentioned the Age Pension in passing. Here is what it actually is, and what *enough*
does with it.

### What it is

The Age Pension is money the government pays older Australians who don't have a lot of income or
assets already. It isn't automatic and it isn't the same for everyone — how much you get depends
on what you have ([Chapter 49](#means-test) explains why).

### Who can get it, and when

You can be paid the Age Pension once you turn **67**. *enough* uses 67 for everyone, all the way
through the forecast, because that's today's rule. If a future government changes it, you'd need
to change your guess too.

### How a couple is worked out

For a couple, *enough* looks at both of your money **together**, works out one combined amount,
then splits it in half. That combined test applies **even if only one of you has turned 67** — the
one who has still gets paid, but on the two of you tested together.

### Pip and Jo

Pip turns 67 in January 2038. From that month, *enough* starts paying Pip an Age Pension — about
**{{fig age-pension-first-month-today nearest-1|$1,533}} a month in today's money** to start with. Jo doesn't turn 67 until January 2040. Once Jo
does, the household's Age Pension jumps to about **{{fig age-pension-both-eligible-today nearest-1|$3,856}} a month in today's money** — not because
the rules changed, but mostly because Jo is now eligible too, on the same combined test.

### Where you see it

The Age Pension shows up as its own line in the month-by-month table ([Chapter 65](#years-and-months)), and in the
income picture ([Chapter 66](#income-chart)). *enough* pays it every month, adding up what the real fortnightly
payments would come to.

### What to do next

You don't add an entry to switch the Age Pension on — *enough* works out whether you're owed one,
every month, from your age and your money. [Chapter 49](#means-test) explains the two tests behind the number.

**Where this comes from:** [Who can get Age Pension (Services Australia)](https://www.servicesaustralia.gov.au/who-can-get-age-pension) and [How much Age Pension you can get (Services Australia)](https://www.servicesaustralia.gov.au/how-much-age-pension-you-can-get). In the appendix: [The Age Pension](#sources-age-pension).

## Chapter 49: Why having more can mean getting less: the assets test, the income test and deeming {#means-test}

The Age Pension isn't a flat amount. The more you have, the less you're paid — and *enough* works
this out with two separate tests, every single month.

### The two tests

- **The assets test.** Looks at what you own: super, savings, investments, and anything else
  besides the home you live in. The more you have above a free amount, the more your pension
  shrinks.
- **The income test.** Looks at what you're paid: your wages (see [Chapter 50](#work-bonus)) and an assumed
  amount your savings and investments are earning — even if they're not really earning that.

*enough* runs **both tests every month, on both of you together, and pays whichever gives the
lower amount.** Neither test is skipped, and neither wins by assumption — the number that comes
out is worked out fresh each time.

### Deeming: an assumed rate, not your real one {#deeming}

For the income test, *enough* doesn't ask what your savings, shares, managed funds, offset
account or account paying you a super income actually earned this month. It assumes a rate
instead — a lower rate up to a threshold, and a higher rate above it. This is called **deeming**.
Money still growing in super, before it starts paying you an income, isn't deemed this way.

Deeming rates are decided by the Minister every so often, with no fixed formula, so *enough* asks
for your own guess about them in the **{{name|Forecast assumptions}}** part of **{{name|Household and settings}}**
([Chapter 15](#assumptions)).

### Your home doesn't count — while you live in it

The house you live in is never counted in either test. Everything else you own is.

### Giving money away {#gifts}

You can't give your money away to get a bigger pension. Centrelink lets you give away only so much
each year without it counting:

- **Up to $10,000 in one financial year** (1 July to 30 June), and
- **up to $30,000 over five financial years** — the year of the gift and the four before it.

A couple gets **one** set of limits between the two of you, not one each. Gifts from either of you,
or from both together, all add up against the same $10,000 and $30,000.

Whatever you give **above** those limits still counts as yours, for **five years from the day of
the gift**. It counts twice over: in the **assets test**, as if it were still in the bank, and in
the **income test**, because it is deemed to earn the same assumed rate as your savings. After five
years it stops counting. The limits are fixed amounts in the law and don't rise with prices.

To tell *enough* about a gift, add a **{{name|Give money away}}** entry:

- **{{name|From}}** — the day of the gift.
- **{{name|Owner}}** — who gave it: one of you, or **{{name|Household}}** if you gave it together.
- **{{name|Amount given, as it will be on the day $}}** — like a one-off expense, the actual dollars
  on the day, not today's money.

The money leaves your cash in that month. From the next month, any part over the limits shows up
in **{{name|Years and months}}** as **{{name|Gifts still counted by Centrelink}}**, beside what you own, and
as its own line on the wealth chart. It's not added to what you own — you really did give it away —
but the Age Pension is worked out as if you still had it.

**Gifts you've already made count too.** If you gave money away in the five years before your
plan's "as at" date, add each gift with its real, earlier date. The money is already gone from the
balances you entered, so *enough* won't take it out again — but the part over the limits still
counts against your pension until its five years are up.

**What *enough* doesn't do here.** Centrelink has some exceptions where a gift doesn't count — for
example paying for a *granny flat* right to live in someone else's home, money put into a special
disability trust for a family member, or a gift given back to you. *enough* doesn't know about any
of these: it counts every gift. If one of them applies to you, your real pension could be higher than
the forecast shows.

**Selling something cheap counts too.** If you sell something for less than it's worth — a car to
your daughter for $1, say — Centrelink treats the difference as a gift. Add a
**{{name|Give money away}}** entry for the difference, on the day of the sale.

**After one of you dies.** Gifts the person who died made on their own stop counting. Gifts the
survivor made, alone or with them, keep counting in full until their five years are up. A gift the survivor makes after the month of the death is tested against
the limits as a single person's: only the survivor's own earlier gifts, and the ones you gave
together, count against them — a gift you gave together counts at half, as Centrelink counts it.

### Pip and Jo

- **In {{fig age-pension-first-month month|January 2038}}**, when Pip's Age Pension starts, the **assets test** is the one holding it
  down — Pip and Jo still have plenty of super and no other investments.
- **By the 2050s**, the **income test** takes over — their assets have fallen, but the super
  accounts paying them an income are still deemed to earn something, and that's now the tighter
  test.

Same household, same rules — just whichever test bites hardest that month.

**Where this comes from:** [Assets test (Services Australia)](https://www.servicesaustralia.gov.au/assets-test-for-age-pension), [Income test (Services Australia)](https://www.servicesaustralia.gov.au/income-test-for-age-pension) and [Deeming (Services Australia)](https://www.servicesaustralia.gov.au/deeming). In the appendix: [The Age Pension](#sources-age-pension).

## Chapter 50: Keeping some of your work pay (the Work Bonus) {#work-bonus}

Once you reach Age Pension age, working doesn't wipe out your pension straight away. This is the
**Work Bonus**.

### How it works

- Each fortnight, the first **$300** of pay from a job (or from working for yourself) is ignored
  by the income test — on top of the free amount the income test already allows.
- Any of that $300 you don't use is **banked**, up to a ceiling of **$11,800**. Fortnights you
  don't work, the balance grows; fortnights you earn more than $300, the extra is drawn from the
  balance before your pension is reduced.
- It only counts in a fortnight you're of Age Pension age, 67, **and** actually paid some Age
  Pension. Any other time — before 67, or a month your pension is nil — the balance just sits,
  neither growing nor being used.
- When your Age Pension starts, a balance under **$4,000** is topped up to $4,000. If your pension
  stops and later starts again, it's topped up again — but not if it was already topped up in the
  last two years.
- It's per person — one person's balance can never cover the other person's pay.
- Money your investments make is never covered by the Work Bonus. That's deemed instead
  ([Chapter 49](#means-test)).

By law, Carer Payment and the Disability Support Pension carry a Work Bonus too, and so does the
old transitional rate of Age Pension. *enough* doesn't model any of those — only the Age Pension.

*enough* works month by month, so the top-up lands from the month **after** the pension starts; the
first month is worked out on the balance you had going in. That can understate your pension by up to
one month.

### Where you tell *enough*

Under **{{name|Household and settings}}**, each person has a **{{name|Work Bonus balance $}}** box. Type what
you've actually banked with Centrelink so far. If you're not on the Age Pension yet, type 0: *enough*
adds the $4,000 itself when your pension starts in the forecast.

Beside it, **{{name|Work Bonus last topped up on}}** is the day your balance was last topped up to
$4,000. Leave it blank if it never has been. If you leave it blank but type a balance above 0, *enough*
assumes it was topped up on your plan's start date, so no top-up comes in the forecast's first two
years — the careful choice, since it can't know better.

### Pip and Jo — a what-if

Pip and Jo both stop working in January 2034, well before Pip's Age Pension starts in {{fig age-pension-first-year nearest-1|2038}}. Each
balance is topped up to $4,000 when that person's Age Pension starts, but there's no pay left to
shelter, so it changes nothing.
**What-if:** if Pip decided to keep doing a few paid shifts a week past 67 instead of fully
retiring, the first $300 of that fortnight's pay would be ignored by the income test, on top of
everything else.

**Where this comes from:** [How Work Bonus works](https://www.servicesaustralia.gov.au/how-work-bonus-works), [Who can get Work Bonus](https://www.servicesaustralia.gov.au/who-can-get-work-bonus) and [Work Bonus balance](https://www.servicesaustralia.gov.au/work-bonus-balance). In the appendix: [The Age Pension](#sources-age-pension).

## Chapter 51: Borrowing against your house (the Home Equity Access Scheme) {#home-equity-access}

The Home Equity Access Scheme lets homeowners borrow against their home from the government,
paid to them as an income. On screen, this entry is called
**"{{name|Borrow against the home — Home Equity Access Scheme}}"**.

### It's a loan, not a pension

This is not free money. The government pays you an amount each fortnight and charges interest
on what you owe — currently **3.95% a year**, compounding every fortnight, so the debt grows even
in a fortnight you draw nothing. Nothing is repaid while you live in the home. The debt is repaid
out of the home when it's sold, or out of your estate when you die.

A **no-negative-equity guarantee** means neither you nor your estate can ever be asked to repay
more than the home turns out to be worth. But getting a new home loan, or changing its terms, from
the day the scheme starts or later voids that guarantee. So *enough* refuses to save the two together.

### Who can use it

Only from Age Pension age, and only if you own your home. Taking out this loan on its own doesn't
give you a Pensioner Concession Card — the card follows an actual Age Pension payment, not this loan.

### What you type

- **{{name|Home value}}**, your own estimate, in today's money — nobody can look this up for you.
- **{{name|How much to draw}}**: either a fixed fortnightly amount, or "{{name|the most allowed}}" — 150% of the
  full Age Pension rate, less any Age Pension you actually receive, which moves automatically if
  your Age Pension does.
- An optional stop date.
- Optional **lump-sum advances**: up to twice a year, and at most half a year's full pension rate
  in total across any rolling year. Taking one lowers your fortnightly payments for the rest of
  that period, spread evenly.

### How it's treated

A lump sum is counted straight away, in both the income test and the assets test, from the day
you get it — *enough* is deliberately cautious here rather than generous. Everything is typed in
today's money, and the forecast raises it with inflation to the date.

### Pip and Jo — a what-if

Pip and Jo pay off their home loan entirely in mid-2038, just after Pip reaches Age Pension
age. **What-if:** once both are over 67 and own their home outright, they could add a
"{{name|Borrow against the home}}" entry for each of them — a loan on top of their Age Pension and their super, never
part of their actual plan, but something you can test for yourself the same way.

**Where this comes from:** [Interest rate for the scheme (Services Australia)](https://www.servicesaustralia.gov.au/interest-rate-for-loans-under-home-equity-access-scheme?context=22546), [How much you can get (Services Australia)](https://www.servicesaustralia.gov.au/how-much-you-can-get-under-home-equity-access-scheme) and [Advance payments (Services Australia)](https://www.servicesaustralia.gov.au/home-equity-access-scheme-advance-payments?context=22546). In the appendix: [The Home Equity Access Scheme](#sources-heas).

## Chapter 52: Moving into a care home {#aged-care}

*enough* can model one person moving into permanent residential aged care. On screen, this entry
is called **"{{name|Move into aged care}}"**.

### Three figures only you can estimate

Nobody can know these before the day they're agreed with a facility, so all three are your own
best guess, in today's money:

- **{{name|Home value on the day of entry}}**, in today's money.
- **{{name|Room price — a refundable deposit, RAD}}**: the room price the facility agrees to. **RAD** stands for Refundable Accommodation Deposit.
- **{{name|Of that, paid as a lump sum now}}**: how much of the room price you pay on entry.

Whatever you don't pay as a lump sum accrues instead as a **daily accommodation payment**,
charged every day it's outstanding. Type $0 to pay nothing up front, or the full room price to pay
it all as a lump sum.

The rate the daily payment is worked out with comes from a formula in the law. The government has announced, but not yet passed into law, that it will replace this rate by July 2027.

### What *enough* works out for you

Each month, on top of the accommodation payment, *enough* adds a basic daily fee, a hotelling
contribution and a non-clinical care contribution. All of this is worked out from your own income
and assets, under the aged care means test. You don't type these.

From 20 March 2027, the hotelling and non-clinical care contributions start only once your means-tested amount passes $78.73 a day (up from $73.73), whatever kind of home you're in ([the rules that set it](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2026L01252/asmade/text)).

### What happens to the deposit

The lump sum is refunded — less a small amount the facility keeps over time — when that person
dies. **Leaving care while still alive isn't modelled**, and a person can only be entered into
care once: a second entry for the same person is refused.

### One entry each

This entry belongs to one named person, never to "the household" — a household doesn't move into
a room. A couple who both need care would add two entries, one each.

### If one of you moves and the other stays home

Once you've told Services Australia, you may be paid as an "illness separated" couple from the
same date — each paid the single rate, but still tested together on your combined money. Add an
"{{name|Illness separation}}" entry from the same date to see that reflected ([Chapter 54](#illness-separation)).

## Chapter 54: When one of you has to live somewhere else {#illness-separation}

Sometimes a couple has to live apart because of illness or the need for care — one moves into a
care home, or somewhere else entirely, while the other stays in the family home. On screen, this
entry is called **"{{name|Live apart because of illness or care}}"**.

### What changes, and what doesn't

From the date you add, each of you is paid the Age Pension **single rate** — more, between the
two of you, than the couple rate you'd get living together. But you're still means-tested
**together**, on your combined income and assets.

For tax you're still a couple, with one difference. The seniors and pensioners tax offset has a
larger amount for a couple living apart because of illness: up to $2,040 each, instead of $1,602.
You get it for the **whole** financial year you start living apart in, even if you move in its last
month, because the law gives it to anyone who was living apart at any time in the year. During
the year, *enough* uses the larger amount from the month you move; when the year is settled in
July, it works out the whole year again with the larger amount, and any tax overpaid comes back.

### What it's for

This reflects a real Centrelink category: an "illness separated" couple, once you've told
Services Australia about the move. Adding this entry in *enough* doesn't grant that status by
itself. It lets you see what your numbers would look like if it applies — so you know what to ask
Services Australia about.

### Adding it alongside a move into care

If one of you is moving into a care home ([Chapter 52](#aged-care)), add this entry from the same date to see
both effects together: the aged care costs, and the higher combined Age Pension.

### Who it's for

This entry only makes sense for a couple — *enough* refuses it for a household of one person,
since there's nobody to be separated from.

## Chapter 55: What happens to the money if someone dies {#death}

*enough* can model one person in a couple dying. On screen, this entry is simply called
**"{{name|Death}}"**.

### Who this is for

A death entry names one person, in a couple — never "the household". *enough* won't model both
partners dying at once. It also refuses a death entry for a household of one person: there
always needs to be a survivor for the money to go to.

### What happens from that month

- **The survivor carries on alone.** From the month of the death, tax and the Age Pension both
  treat them as a single person, not a couple.
- **All of the person's super is paid out as one lump sum**, straight away, to the survivor — and
  it's **tax-free**, because it's paid to a spouse.
- **Investments outside super pass to the survivor** at the same cost base they had. The death
  itself doesn't trigger a tax bill. The real rules have more detail than *enough* models: the
  family home has its own rules, for example. Check with the ATO or an adviser for your case.
  There is one case the law hasn't settled yet; see the next section.
- **Spending doesn't change on its own.** If the survivor would spend differently, add a spending
  change ([Chapter 20](#spending-plan)) from the same date.

### Investments held since before 1 July 2027

From 1 July 2027, anything held on 30 June 2027 is treated as sold and bought back at its value
then ([Chapter 30](#capital-gains-and-franking)). The gain up to that day isn't taxed straight away:
it waits until the investment is really sold. The new law doesn't say what happens to that waiting
gain if the owner dies after 1 July 2027. It might be taxed at the death, or it might pass to the
person who inherits.

*enough* passes it to the survivor, the way the rest of the investment passes. The survivor pays
tax on it when they sell, and still gets the half-gain rule, counted from when it was first bought
rather than from the death. If the tax office later decides it's taxed at the death, the tax would
come earlier than *enough* shows. Check with the ATO or an adviser if this applies to you.

### The bereavement payment

If the couple were both being paid the Age Pension, the survivor also gets a one-off
**bereavement payment**. It's roughly what the couple would have kept getting for a short while,
on top of the survivor's new single pension.

### What's not modelled

This entry doesn't cover leaving money to anyone but a spouse — to children, say. It also doesn't
cover both partners of a couple dying.

### Pip and Jo — a what-if

Pip and Jo have no death entry in their plan. **What-if** Pip died in, say, 2045. From that month,
Jo would become a single Age Pension recipient, would receive Pip's whole super balance as one
tax-free lump sum, and would keep the house, and would keep any other investments at the same cost base as
before.

## Chapter 56: Changing or cancelling the insurance in your super {#insurance-changes}

If a super account holds death or TPD (total and permanent disability) cover, set up in Chapter
11, you can cut it back or cancel it from a date. On screen, this entry is called
**"{{name|Change super insurance cover}}"**.

### What you choose

- **{{name|Which account}}.**
- **{{name|Which cover}}**: death cover, TPD cover, or both.
- **{{name|Share of the cover kept (%), 0 cancels it}}.** 50 halves it.

The premium for that cover falls by the same share — half the cover, half the premium.

### What's refused, and why

- **Raising cover isn't modelled.** A fund asks for fresh health evidence before adding cover
  back, and *enough* has no way to know what a fund would decide, so it won't guess.
- **The account needs that premium already stated** ([Chapter 11](#super-insurance)) — you can't cut back a cover
  that was never there.
- **The change can't be dated before your plan starts.** If it's already happened, state the
  lower premium directly in Household and settings instead.
- **The change can't be dated on or after the birthday that cover ends.** That's the age in
  **{{name|Death cover ends at age}}** or **{{name|TPD cover ends at age}}**, or 70 if the box is
  empty. For both covers, the change is refused once either one has ended, and the message says
  which.

### Two things that happen on their own

Once death and TPD cover are both at 0%, and the account has no income protection, the fund's
insurance administration fee stops too.
Cover also ends for good once an account becomes an account-based pension (starts paying you an
income). A change dated after that point changes nothing, because there's no cover left to
change.

### Pip and Jo — a what-if

Pip and Jo hold no insurance inside their super, so there's nothing to change. Say they had death
cover on Pip's account, and decided it cost more than it was worth once the mortgage was paid
off. They could add a "{{name|Change super insurance cover}}" entry dated from mid-2038, setting the
cover kept to 0%.

**Where this comes from:** the age at which cover ends is set by each fund's own terms, not by law. [Insurance through super (Moneysmart)](https://moneysmart.gov.au/how-life-insurance-works/insurance-through-super) explains that cover can end at a fund's age limit. In the appendix: [Insurance in super](#sources-insurance).

## Chapter 57: Turning the chat on {#chat-setup}

The chat lets you ask *enough* questions about your own plan in plain English. It changes
nothing about your forecast just by existing — it's **off by default**, and every number in
*enough* works exactly the same with it off.

### What's sent over the internet — stated plainly

**When the chat is on, your questions and the forecast's figures are sent to Anthropic. Names and
dates of birth are not.** Ages and money go; who you are does not.

### What it needs before it will ask anything

Find the box called **"{{name|The chat}}"**, near your household settings, and fill in all of:

1. **{{name|Chat on}}** — the tick box that turns it on at all.
2. **{{name|Provider address (https://…)}}** — today, only Anthropic's own address is supported.
3. **{{name|Model name}}** — which of that provider's models to use.
4. **{{name|API key}}** — your own password from the provider, typed into a box that hides it as you type.
5. **{{name|Input price}}** and **{{name|output price}}**, in dollars per million tokens. A
   "token" is roughly a short word or part of one, and the provider's own pricing page gives you
   these two prices.
6. **{{name|Most the chat may spend in a month, in dollars}}** — the most, in dollars, the chat may spend across a calendar month.

Press **{{name|Save chat settings}}**. Until every one of the six is filled in, the chat won't ask
anything, and it tells you exactly which are still missing.

### Where your key lives

**The API key is kept in this browser only. It is never saved in your plan file or sent to
*enough*'s own server.** It only ever leaves this computer inside a request straight to the
provider you named.

### What it costs

This is your own account with the provider, not *enough*'s. Each question costs a small amount,
worked out from the two prices you typed and how much text was used. *enough* keeps a running
total for the calendar month and shows it against your ceiling — "{{name|under $1}}" for anything
smaller than that. It refuses to ask anything more once the month's ceiling is reached.

## Chapter 61: Today's money or future money? The switch that changes every number {#todays-money-switch}

Above the chart is a small switch called **{{name|Money shown as}}**, with two choices:

- **{{name|Today's money — what it would buy now}}** (this is switched on when you open the page)
- **{{name|The actual future amounts}}**

This one switch changes *every* dollar figure on the whole page: the chart, the table, and the
sentences in Financial Situation. Nothing about your plan changes when you flip it — it only
changes how the same figures are displayed (see [Chapter 5](#real-and-pretend-money) for what "today's money" and inflation
mean).

### ⚠️ It can look like money has gone missing. It hasn't.

This is the single most common way to get confused by *enough*, so read this carefully.

Take Pip and Jo's own numbers, at the month they retire (January 2034):

| View | Combined super | Combined total |
|---|---|---|
| The actual future amounts | {{fig retire-super-future nearest-1|$964,788}} | {{fig retire-total-future nearest-1|$1,097,431}} |
| Today's money | {{fig retire-super-today nearest-1|$804,989}} | {{fig retire-total-today nearest-1|$915,663}} |

Both rows describe **exactly the same pile of money**. The top row is what a bank statement would
actually say that month. The bottom row is what that same amount of money would buy if you had it
today. It's smaller, because prices go up every year between now and 2034 ([Chapter 5](#real-and-pretend-money)). Flip the switch
and the number on the page drops by more than {{fig retire-total-drop-between-views down-50000|$150,000}}, but not one dollar has vanished from Pip and
Jo's accounts. It's the same money, described two different ways.

If a number on the page ever looks alarmingly smaller than you expected, this switch is the first
thing to check.

### Why today's money is what you see first

A dollar figure fifty years in the future is hard to judge — is {{fig retire-total-future nearest-1|$1,097,431}} in 2034 a lot? Today's
money answers that by translating it into what it would buy right now, which is why *enough* shows
it by default.

### What doesn't change

The boxes you *type into* — your salary, your super balance, how much you spend a year — always
show the real number you typed. This is true whichever way the switch is set. Only the numbers
*enough* is telling you back are affected.

### Where to read more

Underneath the chart, an "ⓘ" button called **{{name|What "today's money" means}}** opens a longer, plainer
explanation. It includes one honest note: every figure is rounded to the nearest dollar on its
own, so a column of them can add up a dollar or two differently from the total printed beside it.
That's rounding, not an error.

## Chapter 62: The big pictures: wealth charts, and what the colours mean {#wealth-charts}

Below Financial Situation sits the main chart, titled **{{name|Everything you own, and what counts for the Age Pension}}**. It
draws your whole forecast, month by month, as one picture.

### The two shaded bands

The chart is filled with two stacked colours:

- **Purple** — labelled **{{name|Protected — excluded from the assets test}}**. Money the Age Pension's
  assets test never counts, such as super that's still growing and hasn't started paying out.
- **Green** — labelled **{{name|Counted for the Age Pension}}**. Everything else: money
  Centrelink does count when working out your pension (see [Chapter 49](#means-test)).

Add the purple and the green together at any point and you get your total wealth at that month.
They are genuinely two slices of one pie — nothing is missing between them, and nothing is counted
twice.

### The dashed blue line: reachable money

Over the top of the two bands runs a dashed blue line, labelled
**{{name|Reachable money — not added to the shaded areas}}**. This is money the household could actually get its hands on right
now. It's drawn as a line, not a third band, on purpose. Some protected money — like super you
can't draw on yet — isn't reachable. Some assessable money — like a pension already being paid —
is. The line crosses back and forth over both bands rather than sitting neatly above or below them.

### The two stepped lines: the assets test

A teal line, **{{name|Full pension up to (assets test)}}**, and a red line,
**{{name|No pension above (assets test)}}**, show the two dollar limits the assets test applies that month. ([Chapter 49](#means-test) explains what
they mean.) Where the green band rises above the red line, that stretch is lightly shaded. That's
because it's the part of the picture where the assets test alone would leave no pension at all.

### The dashed vertical lines: events

Thin dashed vertical lines mark things happening in the plan, each in its own colour, with a caption
naming it. Pip and Jo's chart has three lines: a **green** one in June 2038 marking their home loan
being cleared, and two **grey** ones — January 2038 and January 2040 — marking the month each of
them reaches Age Pension age. Other colours mark other things: brown for a *Shortfall Age* (the month
spending outruns money), pink for a *Pension-Only Age*, and so on. Chapters [19](#shortfall) and [48](#age-pension) explain those
in the context they matter.

### Flat and empty is not a mistake

If one of the bands sits at zero for years on end, that's your real shape, not a fault in the chart
— nothing here hides or smooths over a genuinely empty stretch.

## Chapter 65: Years and months: the big table, and where each number came from {#years-and-months}

The **{{name|Years and months}}** panel holds the most detailed picture *enough* can show you. It shows
every single month of the forecast, as a table, grouped into Australian financial years (July to
June).

### Opening it up

The panel starts collapsed. Open it and you'll see one row per financial year, also collapsed;
click a year's row to reveal its months. Click the small triangle on a month's row to open that
month in full. Each level shows more than the last, so nothing is thrust at you until you ask for
it.

The columns across the top are **{{name|Year and month}}**, **{{name|Opening}}**, **{{name|Net cash}}**, **{{name|Closing}}**,
**{{name|Total super}}**, **{{name|Investments}}**, **{{name|Total wealth}}**, **{{name|Income from super}}** and **{{name|Age Pension}}**. Opening plus
Net cash equals Closing, every month, without exception — that's the one row that must always add
up, because it's your actual bank balance.

### Inside an open month

Open a month and it splits into a **{{name|Cash}}** section and a **{{name|Wealth only}}** section. Cash is
money that actually moved — it's what turns the Opening figure into the Closing figure. Wealth
only is things that changed on paper but didn't move any cash, such as your super growing. Keeping
them apart means a real dollar moving out never gets confused with a number simply going up.

### Where a figure came from {#figure-sources}

Next to nearly every figure sits a small "ⓘ" button. Open it and you'll see a short label saying
where that figure came from, and — for most lines — a second label saying which of your entries, if
any, caused it. There are only ever a few possible labels, so you always know which one you're
looking at:

- **{{name|Set by law}}** — a rule in tax, super, or Age Pension law decided this figure, not you. Pip's
  income tax each month is labelled this way.
- **{{name|Not set by law — this comes from your own plan}}** — this figure is simply what you told *enough*.
  Pip and Jo's salaries, and their {{fig spend-from-now nearest-1|$70,000}}-a-year spending figure, are labelled this way.
  Pip's Account-Based Pension payments are labelled this way too: open the ⓘ and it shows the
  working behind the legal minimum — Pip's age and the percentage that age attracts, the balance it
  was worked out on — and says so when a payment includes more than the minimum, drawn because the
  household needed it.
- **{{name|Your own choice, within limits set by law}}** — a transition to retirement pension paid at a
  drawdown rate you chose. The rate is yours; the legal minimum and the 10% maximum around it are not.

Beside the "where it came from" label, most lines also carry **{{name|Stated by these entries}}** (and
name them). Others carry **{{name|No entry states this figure}}** if *enough* worked the amount out
itself rather than reading it off something you typed.

Occasionally you'll also see a note that a figure was
**{{name|Borrowed from an earlier year — no figure exists for this one}}**. Pip's income tax in September 2026 carries exactly this note. The
government hadn't yet published one particular tax threshold for that financial year when this book
was written, so *enough* carried forward last year's figure rather than guess ([Chapter 79](#rules-updates) explains
how *enough* keeps up with the rules).

None of this changes any figure. It's the working behind it, one click away, so a number can be
trusted for a reason and not just because it's printed on the page.

## Chapter 66: Where the money comes from: the income picture {#income-chart}

Below the main chart is a second chart, titled **{{name|Money coming in and going out, by financial year}}**.
Where the first chart ([Chapter 62](#wealth-charts)) shows what you *own*, this one shows what *moves* — money coming
in and money going out, year by year.

### Reading the bars

Each financial year gets one bar. Money coming in stacks upward from zero; money going out stacks
downward. Every kind of money gets its own colour, and only the kinds that actually occur in your
plan appear. Pip and Jo's chart shows seven: their pay, the Age Pension, income from super,
investment income, their spending, their mortgage payments, and tax.

### The shortfall bar

If a year asks for money the plan doesn't have, that amount is drawn as its own bright red bar,
never folded into "income" and never smoothed away. A shortfall bar is bad news exactly because it
means a month that year wasn't fully paid for (see [Chapter 19](#shortfall)). Pip and Jo's plan has no shortfall
bar anywhere. Their own money and the Age Pension cover everything they plan to spend, right
through to the last month the forecast covers.

### What this chart doesn't do

It always shows the plan you're currently viewing — not a comparison of several plans, which is the
job of the Compare view on the chart above it ([Chapter 63](#chart-views)). It also groups money by what it actually
is — pay, pension, spending, and so on — not by which account paid for it. Because of this, a
partial financial year at the start or end of the forecast is labelled as partial, rather than
looking like an ordinary, shorter year.

## Chapter 67: The little "i" buttons: getting help on any box {#info-buttons}

All through *enough*, you'll see a short line of text ending in a small circled "ⓘ". This is an
**info button**: click it, and a fuller explanation opens underneath, in the same spot.

### Why it works this way

Every figure on the page could, in principle, come with several sentences explaining exactly how it
was worked out. Printed open, all the time, that much explanation would bury the numbers it's meant
to support. So *enough* keeps the short version on the page and folds the rest away, one click from
anywhere you need it.

Info buttons are never open when the page loads — you always choose to open one. Clicking again
closes it. They work with a keyboard as well as a mouse or a touchscreen, because they're built from
an ordinary web control rather than anything invented for this app.

### Where you'll find them

- Beside column headings in the **{{name|Years and months}}** table, such as **{{name|Investments}}** and
  **{{name|Total wealth}}**, explaining exactly what's counted in each.
- Beside every figure inside an opened month, explaining where it came from ([Chapter 65](#years-and-months)).
- Under the main chart, as **{{name|What "today's money" means}}**, spelling out the switch from [Chapter 61](#todays-money-switch)
  in full, and as **{{name|More about this chart}}**, explaining the dashed reachable-money line from Chapter
  62.
- Beside any caveat — a note that a figure is borrowed from an earlier year, or less certain than
  usual.

### How to use them

If a word, a figure, or a label on the screen doesn't make sense, look for its "ⓘ" first. It says,
in a line or two, what that box does. The rules and figures behind it are in this book, not in the
box. Most boxes end in a **{{name|Read more}}** link naming the chapter that explains them, which opens
in its own tab. The **{{name|User guide}}** link at the top of the page opens this book too.

## Chapter 78: Keeping your plan safe {#plan-file}

Everything you type into *enough* — your family, your money, your plan — is saved into one file
on your own computer. This chapter is about that file: where it lives, how safely it's written,
and what you should do to protect it yourself.

### Where your plan lives

*enough* keeps your plan in a file called `plan.json`, inside a folder called `.enough` in your
own user folder (on a Mac, that's usually something like `/Users/yourname/.enough`). The dot at
the front of the folder's name means most computers hide it by default, so you may need to turn on
"show hidden files" to find it yourself. If you're not sure where yours is, ask whoever set
*enough* up for you — they may have chosen a different spot.

If you use the *Transactions* screen ([Chapter 32](#transactions)), your bank history is saved alongside it, in a
second file in the same folder called `ledger.json`.

### How a save is written

Every time you press a button that saves — adding an entry, changing a setting, ticking a box —
*enough* writes the whole plan out again. It does this carefully: it writes the new file under a
spare name first, then swaps it in for the old one in one single step. That means if your computer
loses power or crashes halfway through a save, you're left with either the old file or the new
one — never a broken, half-written one.

If a save can't be written at all — say, the disk is full — *enough* tells you so on screen. Your
last-saved plan is left exactly as it was.

### Backing it up yourself

*enough* does not back your plan up for you. There is no copy kept anywhere else, and no way to
undo the past — so if the `.enough` folder is lost, so is your plan.

The safest habit is a simple one: every so often, **copy** the whole `.enough` folder —
`plan.json`, `ledger.json` and anything else in it — somewhere else. A USB stick, another folder,
or a cloud drive all work, because it's an ordinary copy of an ordinary file.

### Pip and Jo

Pip copies the `.enough` folder onto a USB stick every few months. That way, if their computer
ever breaks, their plan — years of entries, their scenarios, their whole household — isn't lost
with it.

It's worth doing this **before** trying anything big and hard to undo, like [Chapter 82](#starting-again)'s
"{{name|Start again}}."

## Chapter 79: How *enough* keeps up with the rules {#rules-updates}

*enough* knows a lot of Australian rules: tax scales, Age Pension rates and thresholds, super
caps, deeming rates, aged care fees. This chapter is about where those numbers come from, and how
fresh they are.

### The numbers that are built in

The government's own published figures are built straight into *enough* — you don't type them in,
and you can't change them. They cover tax, the Age Pension, super and aged care, and they're
refreshed roughly once a year. The Age Pension's own rates change on 20 March and 20 September
every year, and most other figures are refreshed against that same 20 September date.

You'll never find these numbers in a box you can edit. If a screen tells you a figure is
**"{{name|Set by law}}"**, it means the number comes from these built-in rules, not from anything
you typed. (You'll see that exact phrase in [Chapter 65](#years-and-months)'s Years and months table.)

### Your own guesses are somewhere else

The **{{name|Forecast assumptions}}** box, in **{{name|Household and settings}}**, is different. Everything in it
is your own guess about the future: how fast prices rise, how fast wages rise, how much your
investments grow, and so on. *enough* keeps these two kinds of number in separate places on
purpose, so a figure you're allowed to change never gets mixed up with one you're not.

### When even "the law" is really a guess

The government hasn't published rules for every year that far ahead — nobody has. Beyond the years
it has actually published, *enough* has to forecast some figures itself, using your own guesses
from the Forecast assumptions box. How fast wages grow decides two things: how far the Age
Pension's basic rate can rise, and how far the yearly limit on extra super contributions can rise.
*enough* is upfront about this on the little "i" buttons beside those boxes ([Chapter 67](#info-buttons)) — they say
plainly when a figure shown is a forecast rather than the law.

### Seeing the rules *enough* is using {#rules-in-force}

Open **{{name|Household and settings}}**, then **{{name|The rules in force}}**, just below your
Forecast assumptions. It lists the main figures the law sets — tax rates, the Age Pension, super
caps, aged care fees and more — as they stand on the day your figures are from
([Chapter 14](#as-at-date)). So they match what the Years and months table labels
**{{name|Set by law}}** for that month. You can read them, but not change them.

Each line gives the figure and the date it started. If the law has already set a new figure for a
later date, the line also says when it changes and to what. For example, the lowest tax rate drops
on 1 July 2027. A few lines say a little more:

- **"none yet"** means the rule is law but hasn't started by your date. The line says when it starts.
- **"the 2025-26 figure; the 2026-27 one is not published yet"** means the government hasn't
  released this year's figure yet, so *enough* uses last year's.
- **"the 2027-28 scale, which the law keeps for later years"** means the law sets these tax rates
  for 2027-28 and every year after it, so a later date still shows them.

The top line says when the rules were last checked against the law. That's the same date as at the
end of [Chapter 85](#sources). Each area (Tax, Super, and so on) has its own little "i" button that
opens the list of sources for it.

Some figures aren't listed. The government raises some figures every year, like the super caps and
the Age Pension. Those are listed only up to the last one it has published. After that, *enough*
raises them itself using your Forecast assumptions, as the section above explains, so they aren't
"the law" any more. Rules that are words rather than numbers, and the small steps inside a
calculation, aren't listed either.

### Pip and Jo

Most of Pip and Jo's early years use the government's own published rules. But their forecast runs
until Jo turns 100, in {{fig last-year nearest-1|2073}} — decades past anything the government has
actually announced. Plenty of their later Age Pension and super-cap figures are really *enough*'s
own forecast from Pip and Jo's wage-growth guess, not the law.

**Where this comes from:** [Social security indexation (DSS)](https://www.dss.gov.au/income-support-payments/social-security-indexation) and [Rates list, 20 September 2026 (DSS)](https://www.dss.gov.au/system/files/documents/2026-08/rates-list-20-september-2026.pdf). In the appendix: [The Age Pension](#sources-age-pension) and [Super and contributions](#sources-super).

## Chapter 80: When *enough* says no {#refusals}

Sometimes, when you try to add something to your plan, *enough* refuses it. This isn't a bug —
it's *enough* telling you, in plain terms, that what you asked for doesn't add up, or isn't
something it knows how to work out.

### What happens when you're refused

*enough* shows you a message, right where you were typing, explaining why. You can read it, change
what you typed, and try again. Nothing about a refusal is hidden or silent.

### Nothing is saved

This is the most important rule in this chapter: **if *enough* says no, nothing is written to
your plan file.** Your plan stays exactly as it was before you tried. You haven't broken anything
and you haven't lost anything — you just haven't added the thing you tried to add, yet.

### A few real examples

- **Asking for more insurance cover inside super.** A real insurer would want fresh health
  questions answered before raising your cover — *enough* has no way to guess the answer, so it
  refuses. You can still lower or cancel cover ([Chapter 56](#insurance-changes)).
- **A home loan repayment too small to cover the interest.** If your stated repayment doesn't even
  cover what the bank charges each month, the loan would never shrink — so rather than pretend,
  *enough* refuses it.
- **Putting more into super than the year's limit allows.** Each year has a legal cap on how much
  can go in at the concessional 15% tax rate. Go over it with a salary sacrifice and *enough*
  refuses, and tells you to state a smaller amount.
- **Buying a new home you haven't sold your old one for.** *enough* can follow a sale followed by
  a purchase, but not a purchase out of nowhere, and not a second purchase after that.
- **Dating a change too early, or too late.** Changing insurance cover to a date before your plan
  even starts, or to a date on or after the birthday that cover ends (70 unless you typed an earlier age), when
  that cover would already have ended, is refused. There's nothing left, at that date, for the change to act on.

### Why this matters

A forecast that quietly accepted anything you typed — an impossible loan, an oversized
contribution — would hand you numbers that look real but aren't. Refusing is *enough* protecting
the honesty of everything else it tells you.

### Pip and Jo

If Pip tried to sacrifice so much salary into super that, added to their employer's own
contributions, it went over the year's cap, *enough* would refuse the entry and say so. It wouldn't
quietly forecast a tax bill nobody had planned for — Pip would need to state a smaller amount
instead.

**Where this comes from:** [Contributions caps (ATO)](https://www.ato.gov.au/tax-rates-and-codes/key-superannuation-rates-and-thresholds/contributions-caps). In the appendix: [Super and contributions](#sources-super).

## Chapter 81: What *enough* doesn't know about {#not-modelled}

*enough* is careful, but it isn't complete. This chapter is a short, honest list of the things it
leaves out on purpose, so you know when to stop trusting a number and start thinking for yourself
— or ask an adviser.

### Things it doesn't model at all

- **Investment properties.** *enough* can hold shares and managed funds outside super (Chapter
  29), but not a rental house: no rent coming in, no claiming a loss against other income if the
  rent doesn't cover the costs, and no land tax or stamp duty. If you own or plan to buy an
  investment property, *enough* simply can't see it.
- **The ups and downs of the share market.** *enough* always assumes one steady growth rate every
  year ([Chapter 6](#guess-not-promise)) — it doesn't try to guess a run of good years followed by a run of bad ones.
  Real investments are bumpier than *enough*'s forecast, in both directions.
- **Rent Assistance.** This is a separate government payment for people who rent rather than own
  their home. *enough* doesn't add it in, even for a household that plans to sell up and rent
  instead ([Chapter 47](#renting-out-the-home)).

### A few smaller limits, worth knowing

- **You can't pay part of your home loan off early, or redraw from it.** *enough* can pay a loan
  off in full in one go ([Chapter 42](#early-loan-payoff)), or change its terms from a date, such as refinancing
  (Chapter 43). But it can't do two other things: pay off part of the loan early while it otherwise
  carries on, or take back money you'd already paid extra off it.
- **One growth rate covers everything a person owns.** If you change how fast someone's
  investments are assumed to grow ([Chapter 31](#return-change)), that one new rate applies to *all* of that person's
  super and other investments together — never to one account and not another.

### Why leave these out?

Building any of these properly means writing a whole new set of rules into *enough* — property tax,
market randomness, a second means test — and getting it wrong would be worse than leaving it out
and saying so plainly. Each one is a real gap, kept on a list, waiting for someone to build it
properly one day.

### Pip and Jo

Pip and Jo have no investment property and no plans to rent, so none of this changes their
forecast today. But if they ever bought a rental house, or one of them wanted to change only their
own super's growth rate and leave the other's alone, that's exactly where *enough* would run out
of road. That's where they'd need to work the numbers out for themselves.

## Chapter 82: Starting again {#starting-again}

Sometimes the most useful thing *enough* can do for you is start over completely: a fresh person,
a fresh plan, nothing carried across. This chapter is about how, and what it costs.

### Where to find it

Open **{{name|Household and settings}}**, and right at the bottom you'll find a button:
**{{name|Start again with a new plan…}}**. Pressing it doesn't write anything yet — it just opens the same
two questions [Chapter 7](#starting-fresh) asked the very first time you opened *enough*: **{{name|Your name}}** and
**{{name|Your date of birth}}**.

### What Finish actually does

Once you've typed both answers, a button appears:
**{{name|Finish — replace my plan with a new one for this person}}**. This is the button that writes.
Press it, and *enough* throws away your whole plan — every entry, every scenario, your whole
household. It replaces all of it with a brand-new plan holding only the name and birthday you just
typed.

**This cannot be undone, and *enough* keeps no backup of the plan it replaces.** If you want to
keep the old one, act before you press Finish — see [Chapter 78](#plan-file) for how to copy your
plan file somewhere safe first.

If you change your mind, there's a **{{name|Cancel — keep my plan as it is}}** button. Pressing that writes
nothing at all, and your old plan is left untouched.

### Your transaction history is asked about separately

If you've been tracking transactions ([Chapter 32](#transactions)), the same screen asks a second, separate
question:
**"{{name|Also delete my transaction history (and the saved bank formats and rules)?}}"** This
box starts **unticked**, meaning your transaction history is kept exactly as it is, even though
your whole plan is being replaced. Tick it, and your transaction history is deleted too, for good,
the moment you press Finish.

### Why you might do this

Starting again makes sense when the household in your plan has genuinely changed. Maybe you're
setting *enough* up fresh for somebody else entirely, or you want to wipe out a plan that's grown
cluttered with old what-ifs and start clean.

### Pip and Jo — a what-if

**What-if:** suppose Pip and Jo decided *enough* had become too cluttered with old scenarios, and
wanted to start completely fresh with just Pip's own name and birthday. They'd back their plan up
first ([Chapter 78](#plan-file)), then press **{{name|Start again with a new plan…}}**, type Pip's details, and press
Finish. Every fact about Jo, the house, the super accounts and the whole plan built up through this
book would be gone for good. This is only a what-if: Pip and Jo's real plan, used everywhere else
in this book, is untouched.
